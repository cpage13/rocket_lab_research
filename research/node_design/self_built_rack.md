# Could Rocket Lab Build Its Own Rack? — NVLink, NVLink Fusion, and the Self-Integrated Fractional Node

*Project: RKLB Space Data Center — feasibility phase. Document date: May 2026.*
*Author: research agent. Hard numbers cross-checked against ≥2 sources where possible; estimates explicitly labeled and reasoned transparently.*

---

## Purpose & relationship to existing project docs

`rack_splitting.md` established two things this doc takes as settled and does **not** re-derive:
(1) a rack is "a rack" because of the **NVLink scale-up fabric**, and the 72-GPU count is a commercial/integration convention layered on a real physical fact (copper NVLink reaches only ~1–2 m); and
(2) a **~36-GPU half-rack inference node is viable** — the binding floor is ~16–36 GPUs of HBM to hold a frontier model, not 72, and steady-state decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and under-uses the 130 TB/s fabric. `rack_internals.md` opened the rack component-by-component (compute trays ≈ half the mass; the copper NVLink spine ≈ 5–8%; ~150–230 kg of ground-DC cabinet + launch reinforcement).

This doc goes one level deeper on a question those two raise but do not answer: **could Rocket Lab build its own rack — and specifically its own fractional (half / quarter) rack — rather than flying a pre-built or lightly-modified NVIDIA rack?** The founder's thesis is that as per-GPU power climbs, a *full* rack will not fit Neutron's mass budget, so RL will *have* to fly fractional racks; early units may be bought-and-modified NVIDIA racks, but the mature architecture is self-integrated. To answer that, this doc dissects (1) how the NVLink scale-up interconnect actually works and whether it is a genuine moat, (2) what NVLink Fusion actually licenses and the realistic self-integration build path, (3) what is genuinely lost vs. a pre-built NVIDIA rack, (4) how much the in-rack fabric matters *for inference specifically*, and (5) what a self-designed half/quarter-rack inference node concretely involves and whether it is realistic for Rocket Lab.

It builds directly on `rack_splitting.md`, `rack_internals.md`, `ai_hardware.md`, `node_mass_model.md`, and `multi_rack_inference.md`.

## Summary

**The question:** A pre-built NVIDIA NVL72 rack is a finished, validated product. Is the value of buying it just "it's ready to go," or is there real, hard-to-replicate engineering — particularly in *how the GPUs communicate* — that a third party like Rocket Lab cannot reproduce? And what does it actually take to self-integrate a fractional rack?

**Headline findings:**

- **The NVLink scale-up fabric is a *partial* moat, and the moat is concentrated in one specific place: the long-reach SerDes.** The in-rack fabric has three replicable layers and one hard one. Replicable: the **copper backplane** (passive twinax cartridges — Amphenol already designs and builds them as a merchant component); the **liquid-cooled mechanical/power integration** (multiple ODMs — Supermicro, Lenovo, Colfax, 2CRSi — already build NVLink systems); and **buying the GPUs/superchips themselves**. The hard layer is **NVLink 5/6 itself — the 200 Gb/s-class long-reach SerDes and the NVSwitch ASIC** — which NVIDIA **explicitly does not license**. NVLink-*C2C* (the short-reach chip-to-chip variant) is licensed; NVLink-5 (the long-reach, rack-scale variant) is not ([irrationalanalysis](https://irrationalanalysis.substack.com/p/nvlink-fusion-jensen-murders-ualink), [ServeTheHome](https://www.servethehome.com/nvidia-announces-nvlink-fusion-bringing-nvlink-to-third-party-cpus-and-accelerators/)). So you cannot *clone* NVLink — but you do not have to: you can *buy it* (see next point).
- **NVLink Fusion is real and it productizes exactly the founder's idea — with one hard constraint.** NVLink Fusion (announced 2025, partners shipping 2H 2026) lets a third party build a **semi-custom rack-scale system** using NVIDIA's NVLink scale-up fabric, NVLink Switch silicon, and the OCP **MGX rack architecture** — with NVIDIA explicitly providing "all of the components required for full rack-scale deployment ... from rack and chassis to power delivery and cooling" ([NVIDIA NVLink Fusion](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/nvlink-fusion/)). The hard constraint: **every NVLink Fusion node must contain NVIDIA silicon** — you may integrate a semi-custom CPU *or* a semi-custom accelerator, **not both**, and NVIDIA controls the fabric-management software ([ServeTheHome](https://www.servethehome.com/nvidia-announces-nvlink-fusion-bringing-nvlink-to-third-party-cpus-and-accelerators/), [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/cpus/nvidia-announces-nvlink-fusion-to-allow-custom-cpus-and-ai-accelerators-to-work-with-its-products)). For Rocket Lab — which has **no AI silicon and no ambition to build any** — this constraint is *not binding*: RL would use NVIDIA GPUs anyway, so it sits on the "NVIDIA-silicon, custom-everything-else" side of the line that NVLink Fusion explicitly supports.
- **For Rocket Lab the realistic path is not "clone the rack" — it is "buy the electronics, design the airframe."** RL does not need to replicate NVLink at all. The viable build path is: **procure the NVLink-connected GPU subsystem** (compute trays + NVLink switch trays + copper spine — as MGX building blocks via NVLink Fusion, or as an off-the-shelf NVL36/HGX baseboard) and **self-design everything around it** — the load-bearing structure, the launch restraints, the spacecraft-grade liquid loop, the power conversion, the radiator interface. This is **satellite integration of a bought avionics box**, which is precisely Rocket Lab's existing core competency (Photon bus, satellite-integration business). It is *not* "become a server OEM."
- **What you lose by not buying a finished NVIDIA rack is real but mostly *integration labor and validation*, not irreplaceable networking IP — provided the NVLink fabric subsystem is bought intact.** The genuine, hard-to-replicate value is NVIDIA's *system-level validation* — signal integrity across the copper spine at 224 Gb/s lane rates, the NVLink topology, thermal/power co-engineering, and the firmware/Mission-Control software stack. If RL buys the NVLink switch trays + compute trays + spine **as a pre-integrated electrical assembly** and only re-houses it, the networking is NVIDIA's and works; what RL takes on is mechanical, thermal and power integration. If RL instead tried to design its *own* NVLink backplane geometry, it would inherit the signal-integrity validation burden — avoidable, and the doc recommends avoiding it.
- **For inference, the tight 130 TB/s in-rack fabric matters far less than for training — and that is the single biggest reason fractional self-built racks are attractive.** `rack_splitting.md` §2 already showed decode is memory-bandwidth-bound; this doc adds the sourcing. Tensor parallelism needs NVLink and **cannot cross even an 8-GPU node boundary** — but for *inference* a frontier model's tensor-parallel group is typically **TP=8 to TP=16**, which fits inside a *fraction* of a rack ([premai](https://blog.premai.io/multi-gpu-llm-inference-tp-vs-pp-vs-ep-parallelism-guide-2026/), [WillItRunAI](https://willitrunai.com/blog/multi-gpu-llm-inference-guide)). The 72-wide domain's inference value is (a) wide MoE expert-parallelism all-to-all and (b) long-context prefill — and NVIDIA itself is now **disaggregating** these onto separate, independently-sized GPU pools (Dynamo) and separate silicon (Rubin CPX). A half- or quarter-rack carrying a complete TP group plus enough HBM is a coherent inference engine; the wide fabric is a training-and-largest-model artifact.
- **A self-designed half / quarter-rack inference node is realistic for Rocket Lab — as a Phase-2 architecture, ~2031–2033 — and the half-rack is the safer first step.** The half-rack (NVL36-class, ~36 GPUs) is the conservative choice: it is *already a shipping NVIDIA SKU* (`rack_splitting.md` §3), it comfortably holds a frontier model's TP group + HBM, and it roughly halves mass, power, radiator and solar — directly easing the flyability wall (`node_mass_model.md`). A *quarter*-rack (~18 GPUs) is viable for inference only if it still carries enough HBM for the model + KV cache and either keeps the model's full TP group on-node or accepts disaggregated prefill — it is the more aggressive option and pushes harder against the HBM floor. Both are within RL's structures/power/thermal capability; neither requires RL to build silicon or to clone NVLink.

**Confidence: medium-high** on the NVLink-Fusion facts and the "NVLink-5 is not licensed" moat boundary (multiply sourced, including NVIDIA's own pages). **High** on the inference-vs-training fabric contrast (consistent with `rack_splitting.md` and multiple independent sources). **Medium** on the realistic self-integration cost/schedule (no public teardown of a non-NVIDIA-integrated rack-scale node exists; NVLink Fusion commercial terms for a non-hyperscaler are not public). **Medium-low** on quarter-rack viability — it depends on frontier-model HBM footprint and TP-group width, both partly non-public.

## 1. How the NVLink scale-up interconnect actually works — and where the moat is

`rack_splitting.md` §1 established *that* the rack is the NVLink scale-up domain. This section opens the fabric itself, because the moat question turns entirely on **which layer of it is hard to replicate**.

### 1.1 The four physical layers of the in-rack NVLink fabric

An NVL72's scale-up fabric is not one thing — it is a stack of four distinct engineering layers, and they are *not* equally hard:

**Layer A — the NVLink SerDes and link protocol (on every GPU).** NVLink 5 (Blackwell GB200/GB300) gives each GPU **1.8 TB/s** of bidirectional bandwidth via **18 links × 100 GB/s**. Each link is built from differential pairs driven by **224 Gb/s-class PAM4 SerDes** — a port is "two 224 Gbps SerDes" giving 400 Gb/s per direction per port ([FiberMall — GB200 interconnect](https://www.fibermall.com/blog/nvidia-gb200-interconnect-architecture.htm), [NVIDIA NVLink](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/nvlink/)). NVLink 6 (Vera Rubin) doubles this to **3.6 TB/s per GPU, ~260 TB/s aggregate** ([NVIDIA NVLink Fusion](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/nvlink-fusion/), [VideoCardz](https://videocardz.com/newz/nvidia-vera-rubin-nvl72-detailed-72-gpus-36-cpus-260-tb-s-scale-up-bandwidth)). This is the layer that is **integral to the GPU silicon** — you get it by buying NVIDIA GPUs, full stop.

**Layer B — the NVLink Switch ASIC (the NVSwitch).** Nine switch trays sit centrally; each tray holds **2 NVLink-switch ASICs**, so 18 switch ASICs form the rack's all-to-all switching layer. A single NVLink-5 switch chip has **36 ports top + 36 bottom and ~7.2 TB/s of bandwidth** ([FiberMall](https://www.fibermall.com/blog/nvidia-gb200-interconnect-architecture.htm)). The switch trays make the fabric **non-blocking and all-to-all** — any GPU reaches any other GPU at full 1.8/3.6 TB/s in one switch hop. The NVLink-6 switch adds resiliency features: **control-plane resilience, the ability to run a partially-populated rack, and hot-swap of switch trays** ([StorageReview — VR NVL72](https://www.storagereview.com/news/nvidia-launches-vera-rubin-architecture-at-ces-2026-the-vr-nvl72-rack)). This ASIC is **NVIDIA-proprietary silicon** — it is *part of* the NVLink Fusion offering (you can *buy* it) but it is not something a third party fabricates.

**Layer C — the copper backplane / NVLink spine.** A **passive** copper cable backplane: **~5,184 coaxial twinax copper cables, >2 miles (3.2 km) of copper**, packed into **4 spine cartridges** with high-density blind-mate connectors (**Amphenol Paladin HD 224G**, each connector integrating 72 differential pairs). When a compute tray is pushed home, "floating connectors at the rear ... automatically 'bite' into the copper backplane," eliminating manual cabling ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/03/21/nvidia_dgx_gb200_nvk72/), [FiberMall](https://www.fibermall.com/blog/nvidia-gb200-interconnect-architecture.htm), [Continuum Labs](https://training.continuumlabs.ai/infrastructure/servers-and-chips/nvidia-gb200-nvl72)). Critically, **the spine cartridge "was designed by Amphenol specifically for the GB200 NVL72 chassis"** — i.e. the physically copper-heavy part is **a merchant connector company's product**, not secret NVIDIA silicon. It is sold as an OEM part; spine and half-spine (NVL36) cartridges even appear on the secondary market ([eBay OEM listings](https://www.ebay.com/itm/277334793017)). The copper layer is **passive and replicable** — its difficulty is signal-integrity engineering, not unobtainium.

**Layer D — system validation, firmware and software.** Topology configuration, link training, the NVLink fabric manager, NVIDIA Mission Control. This is invisible but real, and it is bundled with NVIDIA's product / NVLink Fusion engagement.

### 1.2 So where is the moat? — the long-reach SerDes, and nothing else hard

The moat is **narrow and specific**. NVIDIA's own program boundary makes it explicit:

> **NVLink-C2C (short-reach, chip-to-chip, on a single package/board) is licensed. NVLink-5 (the long-reach, ~200 Gb/s rack-scale SerDes) is *not* licensed** ([irrationalanalysis](https://irrationalanalysis.substack.com/p/nvlink-fusion-jensen-murders-ualink)).

NVIDIA instead sells a **"fusion chiplet" — an asymmetric re-timer with NVLink-C2C on one end and NVLink-5 on the other** ([irrationalanalysis](https://irrationalanalysis.substack.com/p/nvlink-fusion-jensen-murders-ualink)). The long-reach capability is therefore "concentrated exclusively within NVIDIA's proprietary hardware that they manufacture and distribute." Why this layer specifically is hard: a long-reach SerDes must overcome severe backplane signal degradation — reflections, insertion loss across ~1–2 m of twinax — requiring heavy DSP equalization (on the order of **40–80 FFE taps vs. ~15–30 for short-reach**) ([irrationalanalysis](https://irrationalanalysis.substack.com/p/nvlink-fusion-jensen-murders-ualink)). That is genuine, multi-year analog/mixed-signal IP.

**But — and this is the key point for Rocket Lab — the moat being narrow does not block RL, because RL has no reason to cross it.** The moat stops a *competitor accelerator vendor* (AMD, a hyperscaler ASIC team) from cloning NVLink to build a rival ecosystem. Rocket Lab is not an accelerator vendor. RL's plan is to fly **NVIDIA GPUs**. As long as RL *buys* the NVLink fabric (Layers A, B, C) rather than cloning it, the un-licensed long-reach SerDes is simply something RL purchases inside the GPU + switch + spine subsystem. **The moat is a moat against would-be NVLinks; it is a turnstile, not a wall, for someone who pays the NVIDIA toll and only wants to repackage the result.**

The honest qualifier: there *is* an emerging open alternative — **UALink** (AMD/Broadcom-led open scale-up standard; v2.0 spec issued April 2026). But UALink is **not production-ready**: little or no v1.0-compliant silicon exists, the first UALink switch (Upscale AI) is targeted for **Q4 2026** with real deployments slipping into 2027, and "for organizations building GPU clusters in 2026, NVLink remains the only production-ready option" ([HPCwire](https://www.hpcwire.com/2025/12/02/upscale-ai-eyes-late-2026-for-scale-up-ualink-switch/), [The Register — UALink 2.0](https://www.theregister.com/2026/04/07/ualink_2_specs/)). UALink is worth tracking as a Phase-2+ option (it would let RL build a scale-up fabric *without* NVIDIA at all) but it is not a 2026–2028 build path.

## 2. Off-the-shelf self-integration — what can be bought, and the realistic build path

### 2.1 The components are all buyable — through three distinct channels

| Building block | Can a competent third party obtain it? | Channel / evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **GPUs / Grace-Blackwell or Vera-Rubin superchips** | Yes | Sold inside HGX baseboards and MGX trays; ODMs (Supermicro, Lenovo, Colfax, 2CRSi, GIGABYTE) build to order ([ServeTheHome — Supermicro B300](https://www.servethehome.com/checking-out-the-supermicro-nvidia-b300-solutions-and-what-it-takes-to-build-an-ai-factory/)). |
| **NVLink switch silicon (NVSwitch ASIC / switch trays)** | Yes — via NVLink Fusion or as part of an NVL36/NVL72 SKU | NVLink Switch Chips are an explicit NVLink Fusion building block ([NVIDIA NVLink Fusion](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/nvlink-fusion/)). |
| **Copper NVLink spine / backplane cartridges** | Yes — Amphenol merchant part | Amphenol designs/builds the Paladin-HD spine cartridges; OEM parts exist on the open market ([eBay](https://www.ebay.com/itm/277334793017)). |
| **NICs (ConnectX-8 / BlueField, for scale-out / laser-comms gateway)** | Yes | Standard NVIDIA networking products; also listed under NVLink Fusion ([NVIDIA NVLink Fusion](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/nvlink-fusion/)). |
| **Liquid cooling (cold plates, manifolds, CDU, quick-disconnects)** | Yes — and routinely customized | ODMs already deliver "complete solutions for liquid cooling, network topology and cabling, power delivery, and thermal management" as building blocks ([ServeTheHome](https://www.servethehome.com/checking-out-the-supermicro-nvidia-b300-solutions-and-what-it-takes-to-build-an-ai-factory/)). For space the loop is re-engineered anyway (radiator, not chiller) — see `ai_hardware.md` §5. |
| **The MGX rack-scale architecture itself** | Yes — explicitly licensable | NVLink Fusion gives partners the **OCP MGX rack-scale server architecture** plus an ecosystem supplying "all of the components required for full rack-scale deployment ... from rack and chassis to power delivery and cooling" ([NVIDIA NVLink Fusion](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/nvlink-fusion/)). |

**Every physical building block is procurable.** The constraint is not "can you buy the parts" — it is the NVLink Fusion *node rule* and the commercial terms.

### 2.2 What NVLink Fusion actually allows — precisely

NVLink Fusion has **two supported configurations**, and exactly one hard rule:

- **Config 1 — semi-custom CPU + NVIDIA GPU:** a third-party CPU connects to NVIDIA GPUs over **NVLink-C2C** (the licensed short-reach variant). Used by Fujitsu (Monaka) and Qualcomm.
- **Config 2 — NVIDIA CPU + semi-custom accelerator:** a third-party accelerator embeds **NVIDIA's NVLink-5/6 chiplet** and joins the NVLink scale-up fabric alongside NVIDIA CPUs. Used by accelerator partners (Marvell, MediaTek, Alchip, Astera Labs).
- **The hard rule:** "you can use it to integrate a semi-custom CPU *or* a semi-custom GPU, **but not both**" — **every NVLink Fusion node must contain NVIDIA silicon**, and NVIDIA "controls the critical software that manages these connections" ([ServeTheHome](https://www.servethehome.com/nvidia-announces-nvlink-fusion-bringing-nvlink-to-third-party-cpus-and-accelerators/), [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/cpus/nvidia-announces-nvlink-fusion-to-allow-custom-cpus-and-ai-accelerators-to-work-with-its-products)).

**Why this rule is not binding for Rocket Lab.** The "not both" rule exists to stop a partner building a *fully non-NVIDIA* NVLink system. Rocket Lab's design intent is to fly **NVIDIA GPUs and (almost certainly) NVIDIA Grace/Vera CPUs** — RL builds rockets and satellites, not AI accelerators or server CPUs, and has shown no intent to. RL therefore wants the configuration NVLink Fusion *most* directly enables: **all-NVIDIA compute silicon, custom everything-around-it.** In fact RL does not even need the "semi-custom" part of NVLink Fusion — it needs the *other* half of the program: the licensed **MGX rack-scale architecture and component ecosystem** that lets a non-OEM assemble a rack-scale system from NVIDIA building blocks. NVLink Fusion's relevance to RL is **"NVIDIA will sell you the rack-scale building blocks and the integration architecture"** far more than "you can substitute custom silicon."

### 2.3 The realistic build path for a self-integrated half / quarter-rack

The path is **not** "design a rack from a clean sheet of silicon." It is a graded sequence, and `rack_splitting.md` §6.2 already places it as a Phase-2 (~2031–2033) move. Concretely:

1. **Phase 1 (baseline, today → ~2030): buy and space-modify a pre-built NVIDIA rack or half-rack.** Fly an NVL72 or NVL36 SKU, space-modified per `node_mass_model.md` §2 (strip fans/castors, add cold-plate coverage and launch reinforcement). Lowest risk, fastest, fully validated. The founder's own staged thesis — "early on it may buy and modify an NVIDIA rack" — is this phase.
2. **Phase 2a (the realistic self-integration step): buy the NVLink fabric subsystem as a pre-integrated electrical assembly; self-design the airframe.** Procure the **compute trays + NVLink switch trays + copper spine** as MGX building blocks (via NVLink Fusion) or take the electrical guts of an NVL36. Rocket Lab then designs, from scratch and around the orbital constraint: the **primary load-bearing structure** (launch loads, not a data-center floor); **tray/switch restraints** for launch vibration and shock; the **spacecraft liquid loop** (pump, accumulator, radiator interface — no chiller, no facility water); **power conversion** (solar-array DC → the rack's ~50 V DC bus, replacing the AC power shelves entirely); and the **radiator and deployment structure**. This is **satellite integration of a bought avionics subsystem** — RL's existing competency. The NVLink fabric itself is NVIDIA's, bought intact, and works.
3. **Phase 2b (only if justified): self-design the fractional NVLink topology.** Designing RL's *own* backplane geometry for, say, an 18-GPU quarter-rack would let RL optimize mass/thermal layout — but it inherits the **224 Gb/s signal-integrity validation burden**. This is the one step that genuinely strays toward "becoming a server OEM," and the doc recommends **against** it unless a strong mass case emerges. Better: take NVIDIA's NVL36 electrical topology (a shipping, validated 36-GPU NVLink island) and, if a smaller node is wanted, depopulate it rather than redesign the fabric.

The decisive framing: **for Phases 1 and 2a, Rocket Lab never touches the hard layer.** The copper spine is Amphenol's; the SerDes and NVSwitch are NVIDIA's, bought; the NVLink topology is NVIDIA's, bought. RL's scope is structure, restraint, power conversion, and thermal — exactly the satellite-bus engineering it already does for Photon and its satellite-integration customers. The mass win (`rack_splitting.md` §7, `node_mass_model.md`) comes from *re-housing* — shedding the ~150–230 kg ground-DC cabinet + the launch-reinforcement penalty paid when reinforcing an off-the-shelf frame, and from *choosing the GPU count* — not from re-inventing the interconnect.

## 3. What you actually lose vs. a pre-built NVIDIA rack

Is buying NVIDIA's rack just "convenience," or is there hard-to-replicate value? **The honest answer: the value is real, but it is overwhelmingly *system integration and validation labor* — not irreplaceable networking IP — provided the NVLink fabric subsystem is bought intact.** Itemized:

| What NVIDIA's pre-built rack gives you | Is it hard-to-replicate value, or convenience? | What it costs RL to do without (Phase-2a self-integration) |
|---|---|---|
| **The NVLink scale-up fabric (SerDes + NVSwitch + spine), electrically validated** | **Genuinely hard — but you keep it.** In Phase 2a RL *buys* this subsystem intact. The IP and the signal-integrity validation stay NVIDIA's/Amphenol's. **Not lost.** | Nothing — it is procured. The only way to "lose" it is to self-design the backplane (Phase 2b), which the doc advises against. |
| **System-level thermal + power + signal co-engineering of the *whole cabinet*** | Real value, and **partly lost** — but the ground-DC version is the *wrong* design for space anyway. | RL must do spacecraft-grade thermal/power integration regardless (radiator not chiller, solar DC not grid AC — `ai_hardware.md` §4–5). NVIDIA's ground integration is not reusable in orbit. So this is "work RL must do anyway," not "value forfeited." |
| **Mechanical integration: cabinet, blind-mate alignment, ~6,000 lb mating-force handling, EIA-310 frame** | Convenience, and ground-optimized. | RL re-designs the structure for launch loads. This is the ~150–230 kg the project *wants* to shed (`rack_internals.md` §1, `node_mass_model.md` §2) — losing the ground cabinet is a *benefit*. |
| **Firmware / NVLink fabric manager / Mission Control software** | Real value, **retained** if buying NVIDIA fabric silicon. | The fabric software ships with the NVLink switch silicon / NVLink Fusion engagement. Retained. |
| **"It works on day one" — validated, integrated, supportable** | The biggest genuine value: **schedule and risk.** | This is the true cost of self-integration: a multi-year development effort and the loss of NVIDIA's turnkey validation. Quantified as "high integration risk, slow schedule" in `rack_splitting.md` §5.2. |
| **Manufacturer warranty / support umbrella** | Real on the ground; **near-worthless in orbit.** | NVIDIA's standard warranty does not cover self-integrated systems, but a satellite **cannot be serviced anyway** (`rack_splitting.md` §5.2) — so this loss is largely moot for a space operator. A point *in favor* of self-integration. |

**The synthesis.** The advantage of buying NVIDIA's rack is **roughly 80% "it's ready to go" (schedule + validation + risk) and roughly 20% genuinely hard networking engineering** — and that 20% is **fully retained** in the realistic self-integration path because RL *buys* the NVLink fabric subsystem rather than cloning it. There is **no scenario in which Rocket Lab must replicate how the GPUs communicate** — it either buys a finished rack (Phase 1) or buys the NVLink fabric as a subsystem and re-houses it (Phase 2a). The hard-to-replicate networking value is real, but it is **purchasable**, and the un-purchasable residue (NVIDIA's *ground-cabinet* thermal/mechanical integration) is the wrong design for orbit anyway. What RL genuinely takes on by self-integrating is **integration labor, schedule, and program risk** — not a missing piece of irreplaceable interconnect physics.

## 4. Inference specifically — how much does the tight in-rack fabric actually matter?

This is the load-bearing section for the whole self-built-fractional-rack thesis. `rack_splitting.md` §2 reached the verdict; this section sharpens and re-sources it with the specific question: **if the fabric matters far less for inference than for training, fractional self-built racks become far more attractive — is that true?**

### 4.1 The fabric is sized for training; inference uses a fraction of it

- **Training is genuinely fabric-bound.** Continuous cluster-wide all-reduce of gradients every step; tensor-parallel all-reduce after essentially every layer. The full 130/260 TB/s fabric is justified — `rack_splitting.md` §2.1, universal industry consensus.
- **Inference decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, not fabric-bound.** During decode, "the GPU utilization of attention barely improves when scaling batch size due to being bounded by loading KV cache" — the bottleneck is **HBM bandwidth reading weights + KV cache**, not the interconnect ([SemiAnalysis — GTC 2026](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/nvidia-the-inference-kingdom-expands)). "At small batch sizes (1–8), decode is almost always memory-bound." This is the same conclusion `rack_splitting.md` §2.2 reached, now independently re-sourced.
- **The tensor-parallel group for inference is *small* — it fits in a fraction of a rack.** TP must stay on NVLink and "cannot cross a node boundary," but for *inference* the TP group is typically **TP=8 to TP=16** — within NVLink an 8-GPU TP group runs at ~0.92× scaling (~8% comms overhead) ([premai](https://blog.premai.io/multi-gpu-llm-inference-tp-vs-pp-vs-ep-parallelism-guide-2026/), [WillItRunAI](https://willitrunai.com/blog/multi-gpu-llm-inference-guide)). **An 8–16-GPU NVLink island carries an inference TP group with room to spare** — and an 8-GPU HGX baseboard is a mainstream SKU with its own NVSwitch. The 72-wide domain is not needed to *hold a TP group*.

### 4.2 Where the wide domain *does* help inference — and why NVIDIA is disaggregating it away

The 72-wide NVLink domain has exactly two inference-side justifications, and **NVIDIA is physically separating both** off the monolithic rack:

1. **Wide MoE expert-parallelism all-to-all.** Frontier MoE decode "performs best with a wide Expert Parallelism setup"; the 72-GPU domain enables wide EP, and NVIDIA states that "without the 130 TB/s of aggregate bandwidth provided by the NVL72, the complexity and overhead of this communication pattern would make large-scale EP impractical" ([NVIDIA — Wide-EP on NVL72](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/scaling-large-moe-models-with-wide-expert-parallelism-on-nvl72-rack-scale-systems/)). **But the honest caveat — NVIDIA does not publish what fraction of the 130 TB/s wide-EP decode actually consumes.** The independent evidence says EP all-to-all is **moderate** bandwidth: DeepSeek runs 64-way EP across 8 *nodes* over **400 Gb/s InfiniBand** (`multi_rack_inference.md` §2.3) — EP wants a *capable* fabric, not the full 130 TB/s firehose. A half-rack's ~36-GPU NVLink island is a wide EP domain in its own right.
2. **Long-context tensor-parallel prefill.** Scaling the NVLink domain 8→32 GPUs cuts time-to-first-token ~3× for a 405B/122 K-token query (`rack_splitting.md` §2.2). Real, but **prefill is exactly what NVIDIA is moving onto separate hardware** — Rubin CPX, a GDDR7 "context-phase" chip, with prefill and decode on independently-sized pools (`ai_hardware.md` §2.4).

NVIDIA's own inference stack is **un-bundling the monolithic domain**: NVIDIA Dynamo "decouples the compute-bound prefill phase from the memory-bound decode phase," letting the scheduler "allocate specific GPU groups within the rack to prefill ... while other GPUs handle decode" ([Google Cloud — Dynamo on A4X](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/compute/scaling-moe-inference-with-nvidia-dynamo-on-google-cloud-a4x)). And 2026 research is going further still — **Attention-FFN disaggregation** (NVIDIA's AFD; academic *Janus*, *MegaScale-Infer*) splits attention and feed-forward onto *separate* hardware, which "minimizes the scale-up fabric traffic needed for inference, concentrating it on token routing ... rather than continuous model-wide synchronization" ([SemiAnalysis — GTC 2026](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/nvidia-the-inference-kingdom-expands), [arXiv — Janus 2512.13525](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.13525)). The direction of travel is unambiguous: **inference is being decomposed into right-sized pools, not run on one 72-wide monolith.**

### 4.3 The honest counter-point — NVIDIA's revenue argument for the wide domain

NVIDIA does argue the wide domain helps inference *economically*: a Pareto analysis shows "increasing to a 72-GPU domain with NVLink Switch maximizes revenue and performance" vs. 4- or 8-GPU topologies ([NVIDIA — Scaling AI Inference with NVLink](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/scaling-ai-inference-performance-and-flexibility-with-nvidia-nvlink-and-nvlink-fusion/)). This must be weighed honestly: for a *ground* operator selling tokens, a bigger NVLink domain genuinely improves throughput-per-dollar (more flexible batching, wide EP, disaggregation headroom *within* one domain). **But the orbital constraint is different.** Rocket Lab's binding constraint is **mass against Neutron's ~9.5 t reusable budget**, not revenue-per-rack. NVIDIA's Pareto curve optimizes a variable (domain size vs. ground-DC revenue) that is not RL's binding variable. The orbital trade is "fits the mass wall reusably" vs. "maximum tokens per cabinet" — and `rack_splitting.md` §7 already argues a mass-bound project should value the former.

### 4.4 Verdict for §4

**Yes — the tight in-rack fabric matters materially less for inference than for training, and that is the single strongest technical argument for a self-built fractional rack.** For inference: decode is memory-bandwidth-bound; the TP group fits in 8–16 GPUs; wide EP wants a capable but not 130 TB/s fabric; long-context prefill is being disaggregated onto separate silicon. A **half-rack (~36-GPU) NVLink island** comfortably carries a complete inference TP group, is a wide-EP domain in its own right, and holds a frontier model's HBM (`rack_splitting.md` §2.3 floor: ~16–36 GPUs). The 72-wide domain is, for inference, a **training-and-largest-model artifact plus a ground-DC revenue optimization** — neither of which is Rocket Lab's binding constraint. The remaining inference-side risk of going fractional is the **long-context-prefill penalty**, which is real but mitigable (disaggregate prefill, or accept slower TTFT for long prompts) — the same conclusion `rack_splitting.md` §6.1 reached.

## 5. The self-designed half / quarter-rack inference node — what it involves, and is it realistic for Rocket Lab?

### 5.1 What the node physically is

A self-designed fractional inference node is **a satellite payload built around a bought NVLink-connected GPU subsystem**. Its subsystems and who owns each:

| Subsystem | Half-rack (~36 GPU) | Quarter-rack (~18 GPU) | Owner / source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GPUs + NVLink switch + copper spine** | NVL36-class electrical assembly (a *shipping* NVIDIA SKU) | Depopulated NVL36 island, or an MGX sub-assembly | **NVIDIA / Amphenol — bought.** Not RL-designed. |
| **Scale-up fabric** | ~36-GPU all-to-all NVLink island, 1 switch hop | ~18-GPU NVLink island | NVIDIA — bought. Carries the inference TP group + wide EP. |
| **Load-bearing structure + launch restraints** | Clean-sheet, designed for Neutron launch loads | Same | **Rocket Lab** — core competency (Photon bus, satellite structures). |
| **Liquid cooling loop** | Spacecraft pump + accumulator + cold plates (bought) + radiator interface; no chiller | Same, smaller | **Rocket Lab** integrates; cold plates bought. |
| **Power conversion** | Solar-array DC → ~50 V rack bus; the AC power shelves are *deleted* | Same, ~half the power | **Rocket Lab** — RL already builds space power systems and markets solar arrays for space data centers (`ai_hardware.md`). |
| **Radiator + deployment** | ~half the area of a full-rack node (`node_mass_model.md` §4) | ~quarter | **Rocket Lab** — spacecraft thermal/structures. |
| **Laser-comms gateway** | ISL terminal + NIC (`multi_rack_inference.md`) | Same | RL (Mynaric CONDOR) + NVIDIA NIC. |

The half-rack is the **conservative** design: NVL36 is *already a shipping NVIDIA product* (`rack_splitting.md` §3), so RL buys a validated 36-GPU NVLink island and only re-houses it. The quarter-rack is **more aggressive**: ~18 GPUs is not a standard NVL SKU, so RL either depopulates an NVL36 (simplest — keep NVIDIA's validated topology, fit fewer trays) or assembles an MGX sub-rack. The quarter-rack pushes against the **HBM floor** — `rack_splitting.md` §2.3 puts the frontier-model floor at ~16–36 GPUs; at ~18 GPUs of GB300-class 288 GB HBM (~5.2 TB) a current 1–2 T model fits but KV-cache headroom is tighter, and a >~10 T next-gen model would not fit (cross-ref `multi_rack_inference.md` §1, which then meshes nodes by pipeline parallelism).

### 5.2 Networking, cooling, integration — the real engineering content

- **Networking — mostly bought, lightly integrated.** The scale-up fabric (NVLink) is NVIDIA's, intact. RL's networking scope is the **scale-out / laser-comms edge**: connecting the node's NICs to the Mynaric ISL terminal so the node can mesh with other nodes (`multi_rack_inference.md`). For a *single* fractional node serving complete models by replica parallelism, even that is light. **RL does not design a fabric.**
- **Cooling — the genuine RL engineering, and RL is suited to it.** The ground rack assumes a CDU + facility water + chiller; in orbit the loop ends at a **radiator** (`ai_hardware.md` §5). RL must design a closed pumped-loop with accumulator, route it to deployable radiator panels (~3 m²/kW at ~350 W/m²), and handle launch loads on a fluid-filled system. A half-rack at ~70–95 kW needs roughly half the ~370 m² a full GB300 node needs. This is hard but it is **spacecraft thermal engineering**, which RL does — and a *fractional* node makes it easier (less heat, smaller radiator).
- **Integration — satellite integration, not server OEM work.** The defining realization: re-housing a bought NVLink subsystem into a launch-qualified structure with a space power and thermal system **is the Photon-bus / satellite-integration problem**. RL's business *is* integrating bought avionics and payloads into satellites. It is emphatically **not** "design a 224 Gb/s backplane" — that stays NVIDIA's/Amphenol's.

### 5.3 Is it realistic for Rocket Lab?

**Yes — as a Phase-2 architecture (~2031–2033), with the half-rack as the first step.** The capability match:

- **What RL has:** satellite structural design and launch qualification; space power systems (and RL already markets silicon solar arrays for space-based data centers — `ai_hardware.md`); spacecraft thermal control; payload integration; an in-house ISL terminal supplier (Mynaric/CONDOR). These are exactly the self-designed-node subsystems.
- **What RL does not have, and does not need:** AI accelerator silicon; server-OEM rack-integration scale; the ability to fabricate NVLink SerDes or NVSwitch ASICs. The realistic path (§2.3) requires **none** of these — RL buys the NVLink subsystem.
- **What RL takes on:** integration risk and a multi-year development of the self-designed node, layered on the satellite bus — the cost `rack_splitting.md` §5.2 flags as "high risk, slow schedule." This is why it is **Phase 2, not Phase 1**.
- **Why the half-rack first:** NVL36 is a shipping SKU; depopulating/re-housing it is far lower risk than a quarter-rack MGX assembly. The half-rack already roughly halves mass/power/radiator/solar (`rack_splitting.md` §7) — capturing most of the flyability-wall benefit at lower risk. The quarter-rack is a later, more aggressive option, justified only if the silicon-power trajectory pushes even the half-rack node toward Neutron's mass wall.

### 5.4 Linkage to the founder's thesis and the flyability wall

The founder's staged thesis is **directionally correct and supported by this research**:

- *"As rack power climbs, a full rack will not fit Neutron's mass budget"* — consistent with `node_mass_model.md` (the 1-rack node is mass-bound at ~5.6–14.1 t vs. ~9.5 t reusable) and the silicon trend (`ai_hardware.md`: Blackwell ~1.0–1.4 kW/GPU → Rubin ~1.8–2.3 kW/die → Kyber racks ~600 kW). Each generation pushes the *full*-rack node harder into the wall.
- *"RL will have to fly fractional racks"* — supported: a half/quarter-rack roughly halves the dominant node-mass lines (radiator + solar, `rack_splitting.md` §7), and for *inference* the fractional rack is technically sound (§4). The honest counter-weight (`rack_splitting.md` §7): a smaller node carries less compute per launch, so more launches are needed — a launch-economics trade this doc does not re-solve.
- *"Early on it may buy and modify an NVIDIA rack; in the future it self-integrates"* — this is exactly the Phase-1 → Phase-2a sequence of §2.3. The research confirms self-integration is **viable** (NVLink Fusion sells the building blocks; the copper spine is a merchant Amphenol part; ODMs prove rack-scale integration is not NVIDIA-exclusive) and that it does **not** require RL to clone NVLink or become a server OEM.

**Bottom line:** Rocket Lab could build its own fractional rack. The realistic build is **buy the NVLink-connected GPU subsystem, self-design the orbital airframe (structure, restraint, power conversion, thermal, radiator)** — which is satellite integration, RL's existing competency. The NVLink scale-up interconnect is a genuine moat *only* at the long-reach-SerDes layer, and that moat is a turnstile for someone who pays NVIDIA's toll rather than a wall — RL buys NVLink, it does not replicate it. What RL loses by not buying a finished rack is integration labor, schedule, and validation risk — not irreplaceable networking IP. For *inference* specifically, the tight 130 TB/s fabric is largely a training-and-largest-model artifact: decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, the inference TP group fits in 8–16 GPUs, and NVIDIA itself is disaggregating prefill and wide-EP onto separate, right-sized pools. A self-designed **half-rack (~36-GPU)** inference node — the conservative first step — is realistic for Rocket Lab, materially eases the flyability wall, and is best carried as a **Phase-2 architecture (~2031–2033)**, gated as `rack_splitting.md` §6.2 specifies and paired with the launch-economics study that doc flags.

## Consolidated answer to the brief's questions

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **How does the NVLink scale-up interconnect work, and is it a moat?** | Four layers: the NVLink SerDes on each GPU (1.8/3.6 TB/s); the NVSwitch ASIC (non-blocking all-to-all switching); the passive copper spine (~5,184 twinax cables, 4 Amphenol Paladin-HD cartridges); and validation/firmware. **Genuine moat only at one layer — the un-licensed long-reach 200 Gb/s SerDes** (NVLink-C2C is licensed; NVLink-5 is not). It is a moat against rival accelerator vendors, not against a buyer who pays NVIDIA's toll and only re-houses the result. |
| **Can the components be bought and self-assembled? What does NVLink Fusion allow?** | Yes — GPUs/superchips, NVSwitch silicon, the Amphenol copper spine, NICs, cooling, and the MGX rack architecture are all procurable. NVLink Fusion lets a third party build a **semi-custom rack-scale system** with NVIDIA's NVLink fabric; the one hard rule — every node must contain NVIDIA silicon, custom-CPU *or* custom-accelerator but not both — **does not bind Rocket Lab**, which would fly all-NVIDIA compute silicon anyway. |
| **Realistic self-integration build path?** | Phase 1: buy/space-modify a pre-built NVIDIA NVL72 or NVL36. Phase 2a: buy the NVLink fabric subsystem (compute + switch trays + spine) as a pre-integrated electrical assembly and self-design the orbital airframe — structure, launch restraint, power conversion, spacecraft liquid loop, radiator. RL never touches the hard SerDes layer. Avoid Phase 2b (self-designed backplane). |
| **What do you lose vs. a pre-built NVIDIA rack?** | ~80% schedule + validation + risk ("it works on day one"); ~20% genuinely hard networking engineering — and that 20% is **retained**, because the realistic path *buys* the NVLink fabric intact. The un-purchasable residue is NVIDIA's *ground-cabinet* thermal/mechanical integration, which is the wrong design for orbit anyway. No scenario forces RL to replicate how GPUs communicate. |
| **How much does the in-rack fabric matter for inference?** | Materially less than for training. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound; the inference TP group fits in 8–16 GPUs; wide EP wants a capable but not 130 TB/s fabric; long-context prefill is being disaggregated onto separate silicon (Rubin CPX) and pools (Dynamo, AFD). The 72-wide domain is, for inference, a training/largest-model artifact plus a ground-DC revenue optimization — not RL's binding constraint. |
| **Is a self-designed half/quarter-rack realistic for Rocket Lab?** | Yes — as a Phase-2 architecture (~2031–2033). It is satellite integration of a bought NVLink subsystem (structure, power, thermal, radiator) — RL's existing competency — not server-OEM work. The half-rack (NVL36-class, a shipping SKU) is the conservative first step and roughly halves mass/power/radiator/solar; the quarter-rack is the more aggressive option, pushing against the ~16–36-GPU HBM floor. |

## Open questions / uncertainties

1. **NVLink Fusion commercial terms for a non-hyperscaler are not public.** Every announced NVLink Fusion partner is a large silicon vendor or hyperscaler (AWS, Fujitsu, Qualcomm, Marvell, MediaTek, Alchip, Astera Labs, Samsung). Whether NVIDIA will engage a mid-size space operator that wants the **MGX rack-scale architecture + NVLink switch building blocks** (but no custom silicon) — and at what price and minimum volume — is unknown. This is the gating *commercial* unknown; it does not change the *technical* feasibility but it bears on the procurement risk `rack_splitting.md` open-question #2 raised.
2. **Can the NVLink fabric subsystem be bought as a stand-alone pre-integrated electrical assembly?** The Phase-2a path assumes RL can procure "compute trays + NVLink switch trays + copper spine" as a coherent sub-assembly to re-house. NVIDIA sells *finished racks* and *MGX building blocks*; whether the exact fractional-island assembly RL wants is a catalog item, or requires an MGX/NVLink-Fusion engagement, is not established. The NVL36-depopulation route (buy a shipping NVL36, re-house it) is the lower-risk fallback and is clearly feasible.
3. **Quarter-rack viability depends on non-public frontier-model parameters.** An ~18-GPU node sits at the bottom of the ~16–36-GPU HBM floor. Whether a quarter-rack holds the *intended-era* frontier model + realistic KV-cache headroom depends on that model's size and TP-group width — both partly non-public (cross-ref `inference_scaling.md` / `multi_rack_inference.md` open questions). The half-rack does not have this exposure.
4. **No public teardown or mass figure for a self-integrated (non-NVIDIA-rack) rack-scale node.** The mass saving from re-housing (shedding the ~150–230 kg ground cabinet + reinforcement penalty) is sound directionally (`node_mass_model.md` §2) but unbacked by a built example. Starcloud self-integrates GPUs/blades but publishes no component masses.
5. **NVSwitch ASIC availability outside a rack SKU.** NVLink Fusion lists "NVLink Switch Chips" as a building block, but whether the *switch trays* are sold separately to a non-OEM (vs. only inside a rack) is not confirmed. Same uncertainty class as #2.
6. **The signal-integrity burden of any RL-designed backplane (Phase 2b) is unquantified.** The doc recommends *against* self-designing the NVLink backplane, but if a future mass case forces it, the 224 Gb/s-class signal-integrity validation effort and schedule are not estimated here — they would be a genuine server-OEM-grade engineering program.
7. **UALink timing could change the Phase-2+ picture.** If UALink matures (production silicon ~2027+), RL would eventually have an *open* scale-up fabric option that needs no NVIDIA toll at all — potentially attractive for a fully self-integrated node. Too immature to plan around in 2026; revisit when UALink switches ship in volume.
8. **Wide-EP decode fabric utilization is still unpublished.** As `rack_splitting.md` open-question #1 also notes, NVIDIA claims wide EP "needs" the 130 TB/s but publishes no fraction-utilized figure. A profiling study on a real frontier MoE model would firm up exactly how much fabric a half-rack's ~36-GPU EP domain sacrifices vs. the full 72.
