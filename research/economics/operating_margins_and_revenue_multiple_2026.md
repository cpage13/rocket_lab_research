# Operating Margins, Gross Margins, and the Revenue-to-Cost Multiple (R): Is R = 1.5 Fair? (2026)

*Research date: 2026-06-01. Prepared for the Rocket Lab orbital AI-inference data center feasibility project. Companion to and partial update of `economics/hyperscaler_margins.md`, `economics/revenue_economics_2026.md`, `economics/gpu_hour_rental_rates.md`, `economics/revenue_per_watt.md`, and `economics/premium_value_case.md`. Supersedes the stale Oracle "30-40% margin" figure carried in `revenue_economics_2026.md`.*

> **Purpose.** The orbital model prices compute revenue as **R times the node's annualized build-and-launch cost** (amortized CAPEX, divided by service life; the orbital model has near-zero opex because solar is free and there is no grid bill). R is a configurable band: **low 1.2, central 1.5, high 1.8**. R = 1.5 means revenue is 150% of amortized cost, which is a **33.3% gross margin** over that cost base. This document answers four founder questions: (a) what an operating margin is and how it differs from a gross margin and from a cost-plus markup; (b) whether rapid scaling by ground operators is compressing their operating margins; (c) what revenue-to-cost (price-to-cost) multiples ground / GPU-cloud operators actually run; and (d) whether R = 1.5 is a fair, defensible central assumption.

> **Reading guide.** Claims are tagged **[FACT]** (company-disclosed or reported 2025-26 data), **[DERIVED]** (our arithmetic), or **[INFERENCE]** (our reasoning, explicitly not an external fact). Hard numbers are cross-checked against 2+ independent sources and cited inline with URLs. Where a number is an estimate for a private or opaque figure it is marked **[ESTIMATE]**.

## BLUF: Is R = 1.5 fair?

**Yes. R = 1.5 (a 33.3% markup over amortized build-and-launch cost) is a fair, defensible central assumption, and is arguably mildly conservative, AND the lens that makes it most defensible is the OPERATING-margin lens, which favors orbital.** The single most important framing point, corrected from the prior draft: **R is a markup over the node's amortized CAPEX (depreciation of the build-and-launch cost over the service life), and because the orbital node has very little opex (no grid power, no water, no land, minimal staff), the orbital gross margin and the orbital OPERATING margin nearly coincide.** That is the structural advantage of orbit: a ground operator's fat gross margin collapses to a thin operating margin after it pays for power, cooling, leases, and a large facility staff, whereas in orbit there is almost no such wedge, so R = 1.5's 33.3% is close to an OPERATING margin, not merely a gross one. **Therefore the primary comparison is to operators' OPERATING margins, not their gross margins.** Against operating margins, the orbital ~33% operating-equivalent is strong: it dwarfs the neoclouds' compressed operating margins (CoreWeave ~ -0.9% FY2025, ~1.0% adj. operating Q1 FY2026; Nebius negative; Oracle's AI-server-rental deals ~16% deal-level and negative on the newest Blackwell chips), and it is comparable to mature hyperscaler-cloud operating margins (Google Cloud 32.9% in Q1 2026, AWS ~35-38%, Azure/Intelligent Cloud ~45%). In other words, orbit can run at a hyperscaler-grade operating margin on a commodity-rental-grade gross markup, because it skips the opex that destroys the ground operators' operating line.

**The honest caveat travels with the lens:** the orbital 33% still omits some real opex (comms / ground-station, mission operations, insurance) and is before financing and tax, so it is an **upper bound** on the true orbital operating margin, but a *much tighter* upper bound than the gross-to-operating gap that separates a ground operator's reported gross margin from its real operating margin (see §1 and Open Question 2). The secondary framing point still holds: as a markup, R = 1.5's 33.3% also sits inside the disclosed *gross*-margin band of the comparable layer (Oracle OCI AI capacity ~32% Q3 FY2026, guided 30-40%; CoreWeave/Nebius ~70%+ reported but depreciation-flattered), so the number is defensible on both lenses. The band edges map cleanly to reality: **R = 1.2 (16.7%) is the thin-operator / Oracle-deal-level / commodity floor; R = 1.8 (44.4%) is the strong-pricing-power / mature-hyperscaler-cloud-operating ceiling.** R = 1.5 is the well-centered midpoint. **Confidence: high** on the operating-margin lens favoring orbital and on the disclosed operator gross/operating margins (multiple primary sources); **moderate** on the precise mapping of R to a lifetime price-to-cost multiple, because R is defined over *amortized CAPEX* whereas operator gross margins are defined over *cost of revenue*, and the two cost bases are not identical (see Open Question 1).

## 1. Gross margin vs operating margin vs net margin: precise definitions

The founder's first question. These three margins differ only in *which costs* are subtracted from revenue before dividing by revenue. Each is a percentage of **revenue** (the denominator is always revenue).

| Margin | Formula | What is subtracted | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gross margin** | (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue | Only **cost of goods/revenue (COGS)**: the direct cost of producing/delivering the service | How profitable each unit of service is *before* running the business |
| **Operating margin** | Operating income / Revenue | COGS **plus** all **operating expenses (opex)**: SG&A, sales & marketing, R&D, admin, rent | How profitable the *core business operation* is, before financing and tax |
| **Net margin** | Net income / Revenue | COGS + opex **plus** interest, taxes, and all non-operating items | What the company actually keeps for shareholders |

Sources: [Britannica Money: Profit Margin Types](https://www.britannica.com/money/profit-margin-types), [CloudZero: SaaS Gross Margin](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/saas-gross-margin/), [The Rich Guy Math: Operating Margin](https://therichguymath.com/operating-margin-guide/), [Wikipedia: Gross margin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_margin).

**What COGS includes for a compute / cloud business [FACT].** COGS (cost of revenue) is the cost incurred in *delivering* the service. For a cloud / GPU-rental operator the cost of revenue typically includes: **the depreciation of the servers/GPUs, data-center lease or power, cooling, networking, and the direct labor and direct costs of operating the facility.** A widely-used practical test: *"Can my customers still use the service if I don't pay that expense?"* If no, it belongs in COGS / gross margin ([CloudZero](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/saas-gross-margin/), [Chargebee: SaaS Gross Margin](https://www.chargebee.com/resources/glossaries/saas-gross-margin/)). The single most contested line is **GPU/server depreciation**: it is a real cost of delivering the service and belongs in COGS, but operators frequently report a "gross margin" that pushes much of it below the line, which is why neocloud reported gross margins look high while true economics are thin (see §2).

**What opex (the gross-to-operating gap) includes [FACT].** Operating expenses are the costs of *running the company* that are not tied to delivering a single unit: **sales & marketing, R&D, general & administrative, executive, and corporate overhead** ([The Rich Guy Math](https://therichguymath.com/operating-margin-guide/), [CloudZero: Margin Analysis](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/margin-analysis/)). Operating margin = gross margin minus these.

**Why this matters for the orbital model, and why the operating-margin lens favors orbital [INFERENCE].** This is the central point of the document, not an aside. The orbital node has an unusual cost structure: **its cost of revenue is dominated by the amortized CAPEX (the depreciation of the build-and-launch cost over the service life), and it has very little opex** (solar power is free, there is no grid bill, no water, no land, no data-center lease, and minimal on-orbit labor). For a terrestrial operator the gross-to-operating wedge is large and is what kills the operating margin: power and cooling, the facility lease, and a sizeable on-site staff all sit between the fat reported gross margin and the thin operating margin. **In orbit that wedge is largely absent, so the orbital gross margin and the orbital operating margin nearly coincide.** Concretely: R = 1.5's 33.3% is close to an *operating* margin for the orbital node, not merely a gross one. This is a genuine structural feature, not a modeling shortcut: in orbit the depreciation IS most of the cost, so R-over-amortized-CAPEX captures the bulk of the real cost base.

The consequence for comparison is decisive. **The meaningful benchmark for the orbital 33% is operators' OPERATING margins, not their gross margins**, because the orbital 33% is already an operating-equivalent. On that lens orbit looks strong: it is far above the neoclouds' compressed operating margins (CoreWeave ~ -0.9% FY2025, ~1.0% adjusted operating in Q1 FY2026; Oracle's AI-server rentals ~16% deal-level and negative on Blackwell, see §3) and comparable to mature hyperscaler-cloud operating margins (Google Cloud 32.9%, AWS ~35-38%, Azure/Intelligent Cloud ~45%). A ground neocloud reports a ~70% *gross* margin and still bleeds at the *operating* line; orbit can report a ~33% gross markup and have most of it survive to the operating line, because there is almost no opex to subtract. Orbit trades a lower gross markup for a far smaller gross-to-operating gap, and the operating line is the one that matters.

**The caveat, kept honest [INFERENCE].** The orbital 33% is an *upper bound* on the true orbital operating margin, because some real opex is not yet in the denominator: comms / ground-station cost, mission operations staff, downlink/bandwidth, and insurance (these are the orbital analogues of a ground operator's facility opex, and they are genuine, see Open Question 2). It is also a pre-financing, pre-tax figure: the venture still nets interest on a large front-loaded CAPEX raise and pays tax below the operating line, exactly the gap `review_economist.md` Findings 1-2 flag for the company-level valuation model. So R = 1.5 is not a bottom-line investor return. But the orbital upper bound is *much tighter* than the gross-margin upper bound that a ground operator's reported number represents, because the missing opex in orbit is a thin slice (comms, mission ops, insurance), not the thick power-lease-staff wedge that separates a ground operator's gross margin from its operating margin.

## 2. Cost-plus markup vs gross margin: the exact relationship (and why R = 1.5 = 33.3% margin)

The founder's model uses R as a **multiplier on cost** (revenue = R × amortized cost). That is a **cost-plus markup**, and it converts to a gross *margin* by a fixed formula. Confusing the two is a classic and costly error.

- **Markup** is profit as a percentage of **cost**. R = 1.5 means a **50% markup** (revenue is 1.5× cost, i.e. cost + 50% of cost).
- **Margin** is profit as a percentage of **revenue (price)**.
- Conversion: **Margin = Markup / (1 + Markup)**. So a 50% markup = 0.50 / 1.50 = **33.3% margin**. ([inFlow: Margin vs Markup](https://www.inflowinventory.com/blog/calculate-margin-vs-markup/), [Patriot Software: Margin vs Markup chart](https://www.patriotsoftware.com/blog/accounting/margin-vs-markup-chart-infographic/), [Calculator Academy: Markup to Margin](https://calculator.academy/markup-to-margin-calculator/)).

This is exactly the relationship the founder already understands and is the arithmetic backbone of R. The full band converts as follows [DERIVED]:

| R (revenue / cost) | Markup over cost | Gross margin (profit / revenue) |
|---|---|---|
| **1.2** (low) | +20% | **16.7%** |
| **1.5** (central) | +50% | **33.3%** |
| **1.8** (high) | +80% | **44.4%** |
| *(reference)* 1.1 | +10% | 9.1% |
| *(reference)* 1.9 | +90% | 47.4% |

**The crucial distinction the founder asked for, in one line:** R is a **cost-plus markup that equals a gross margin** (33.3% at R = 1.5): it is a top-of-the-P&L number measured against the cost of delivering the service. It is **not** an operating margin (which would further subtract SG&A/R&D/corporate overhead) and **not** a net margin (which would further subtract interest and tax). Because the orbital node has minimal opex, its operating margin would be *close to* its gross margin, but at the *company* level the venture still pays financing and tax below the line, so R = 1.5 must not be read as a 33% bottom-line return. R = 1.5 is the cost-plus *gross* markup; the bottom-line return is lower and is the subject of the separate valuation model.

**Read-through for R [INFERENCE].** Three reference multiples bound the question:
1. **Thin/commodity rental layer (Oracle OCI realized, CoreWeave operating today):** revenue-to-cost ~**1.0-1.2×** (breakeven to ~16% margin). This is the floor, and it maps to **R = 1.2 (16.7% margin)**.
2. **Durable owner-operator target (CoreWeave 25-30% long-term op margin):** ~**1.33-1.43×**. R = 1.5 sits just above this, appropriate, because R is a *gross* markup (pre-opex) while CoreWeave's 25-30% is an *operating* target (post-opex), and a gross markup should sit above the operating margin of the same business.
3. **Mature, scarcity-priced cloud (hyperscaler cloud operating margins 33-49%; reported neocloud gross margins ~70%):** ~**1.5-2.0×**. R = 1.8 (44.4% margin) sits at the operating-margin ceiling of mature hyperscaler cloud and well below reported neocloud gross margins.

R = 1.5 (33.3% gross margin, 1.5× revenue-to-cost) lands between the durable-operator-target and the mature-cloud ceiling: a defensible center.

## 4. The scaling effect: is rapid growth compressing operators' margins? (founder question b)

**Yes, but the compression is in OPERATING margin, not gross margin, and it is a timing artifact of rapid CAPEX, not a structural collapse of unit economics. The split is exactly the gross-vs-operating distinction.** [FACT]

**The mechanism [FACT].** When a GPU-cloud operator scales fast, depreciation, lease, and power costs begin *the moment capacity is energized*, but customer workloads take time to fill the new capacity. CoreWeave described this as a timing mismatch that pressures margin during rapid expansion, "especially when new capacity added is large relative to the installed base" ([Futurum: CoreWeave Q1 FY2026](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/coreweave-q1-fy-2026-capacity-constraints-amid-accelerating-ai-demand/)). The result is a dramatic operating-margin compression even as gross margin holds:

- **CoreWeave:** adjusted operating income margin fell from **17.0% (Q1 FY2025) to 1.0% (Q1 FY2026)**, with management explicitly flagging Q1 2026 as the *trough*, while *gross* margin stayed high (~71% FY2025). 2026 capex guidance is **$30-35B against $12-13B revenue** (capex ~2.5× revenue). Management's long-term adjusted operating-income target is **25-30%** ([Futurum](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/coreweave-q1-fy-2026-capacity-constraints-amid-accelerating-ai-demand/), [StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/financials/CRWV/), [tech-insider: CoreWeave $30B capex](https://tech-insider.org/coreweave-30-billion-capex-ai-cloud-2026/)).
- **Nebius:** loss from operations *widened* (to $361.7M in Q1 2026) as it scaled, even as *cost of revenue fell from 49% to 26% of revenue* (gross margin improving from ~51% to ~74%): gross economics improving, operating line still negative on buildout. D&A rose 332% YoY ([Nebius 6-K FY2026, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001513845/000110465926059872/tm2614392d1_ex99-2.htm)).
- **Oracle OCI:** AI-server-rental revenue tripled YoY to ~$900M (T3M ending Aug 2025) but realized gross margin was only ~14-16%, and Oracle *lost ~$100M on the newest Blackwell rentals* specifically (discounting, high power/refresh costs, and variable utilization compressing the new-capacity margin) ([Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/news/4502414-oracles-documents-show-financial-challenges-of-renting-out-nvidias-chips), [Digitimes](https://www.digitimes.com/news/a20251008PD224/oracle-cloud-computing-business-gross-margin-revenue.html)).

**The counter-case: hyperscalers' cloud operating margins are EXPANDING, not compressing [FACT].** The integrated hyperscalers, scaling just as fast, show the opposite: **Google Cloud operating margin nearly doubled from 17.8% (Q1 2025) to 32.9% (Q1 2026)** ([Investing.com: Alphabet Q1 2026](https://www.investing.com/news/company-news/alphabet-q1-2026-slides-cloud-surges-63-ai-investments-accelerate-93CH-4654872)); AWS operating income rose ~23% YoY with margin ~35-38% ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/29/aws-earnings-q1-2026.html)); Azure/Intelligent Cloud held ~45%+. The difference: hyperscalers spread huge fixed software and customer-relationship value over the new capacity and buy silicon at maximum scale, so operating leverage *improves* with growth. The pure GPU-rental neoclouds have no such software moat, so their operating margin is exposed to the raw depreciation-timing wedge.

**Implication for R [INFERENCE].** The scaling effect is a caution about the *operating/net* line, not about R itself. R is a *gross* markup; the disclosures confirm gross economics are robust (Oracle 30-40% guided, CoreWeave/Nebius ~70%+ reported) even while operating margins are temporarily compressed by buildout. **The orbital model is partly insulated from this specific compression** because it has near-zero opex and no ground-power bill, so the terrestrial gross-to-operating wedge (power/lease/large staff) is mostly absent. **But the orbital model is fully exposed to the underlying driver, depreciation timing**, because its CAPEX (build + launch) is enormous and front-loaded and it cannot refresh silicon. This is precisely why `review_economist.md` Finding 1 insists depreciation be charged on *deployed capital* (full straight-line), not on the attrition-weighted live fleet: the same depreciation-timing reality that compresses CoreWeave's operating margin must be honestly charged against the orbital node, or the orbital operating margin is overstated. R = 1.5 is the *gross* markup that sits *above* that depreciation line; it is the right place to set the revenue-vs-cost-of-service relationship, and it does not by itself promise a 33% bottom-line return.

## 5. Verdict: is R = 1.5 fair, conservative, or aggressive?

**R = 1.5 (33.3% markup, 1.5× revenue-to-amortized-cost) is FAIR as a central assumption, and leans mildly conservative for a scarcity-priced, differentiated owner-operator compute product. The strongest argument for it is the OPERATING-margin lens: orbit's near-zero opex means R = 1.5's 33.3% is close to an operating margin, and a ~33% operating margin is hyperscaler-grade.** [INFERENCE, built on §3-4 FACTs]

**Why fair-to-conservative (operating-margin lens first):**
- On the lens that matters, **the orbital 33% is an operating-equivalent, and it is comparable to the best ground operators' OPERATING margins** (Google Cloud 32.9%, AWS ~35-38%, Azure ~45%) while crushing the neoclouds' compressed operating margins (CoreWeave ~ -0.9% FY2025 / ~1.0% Q1 FY2026; Oracle ~16% deal-level and negative on Blackwell). Orbit reaches a hyperscaler-grade operating margin without a software moat, purely because it skips the opex (power, lease, staff) that destroys the ground operators' operating line. This is the central reason R = 1.5 is defensible, and arguably conservative: a hyperscaler-grade operating margin is the *output*, on a markup no richer than the commodity-rental floor.
- As a markup, it also sits **inside the disclosed gross-margin band of the comparable layer.** Oracle OCI's AI capacity is guided to a **30-40% gross margin** and ran ~32% in Q3 FY2026 ([Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366636165/Oracle-expects-to-increase-OCI-margins-by-30-40), [Futurum](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/oracle-q3-fy-2026-earnings-driven-by-oci-ai-infrastructure-demand/)). A 33.3% markup is right in the middle of that range, and *below* the ~70% reported gross margins of CoreWeave and Nebius (which, granted, understate true cost by deferring depreciation).
- It is **above the durable owner-operator operating-margin target** (CoreWeave's long-term 25-30%, ≈ 1.33-1.43× revenue-to-cost). For a *ground* operator a 33% markup would be a pre-opex gross figure sitting above a thinner post-opex operating margin; for the *orbital* operator the 33% is already near the operating line, so landing just above CoreWeave's aspirational 25-30% operating target is exactly where a differentiated, low-opex operator should be.
- The orbital product is explicitly a **differentiated, scarce-attribute** offering (schedule certainty, isolation, zero-grid green; `premium_value_case.md`), not a commodity FLOPs reseller. Differentiated compute earns *above* the thin-rental floor, so anchoring the central case at a 33% markup rather than the ~16% commodity floor is justified by the product positioning the rest of the project builds.

**Where the band edges sit [DERIVED + FACT]:**
- **R = 1.2 (16.7% gross margin)** is the **thin-operator / commodity floor**. It is almost exactly Oracle's realized ~16% deal-level profit on AI-server rentals and CoreWeave's roughly-breakeven-to-low operating reality during buildout. This is the right low edge: it represents the orbital product competing as undifferentiated capacity.
- **R = 1.8 (44.4% gross margin)** is the **strong-pricing-power ceiling**. It sits at the top of the mature-hyperscaler-cloud *operating*-margin range (Azure ~45%) and below reported neocloud gross margins. It represents the orbital product successfully charging a scarcity/attribute premium. This is the right high edge: aggressive but not unprecedented.
- **R = 1.5 (33.3%)** is the well-centered midpoint: neither the commodity floor nor the premium ceiling.

**The one caveat that must travel with R [INFERENCE]:** R is a **markup over amortized CAPEX**, and because the orbital node has very little opex it behaves as an **operating-equivalent margin, NOT a net margin and not an investor return.** The favorable reading (orbital gross ≈ orbital operating) is the right one, but it remains an *upper bound* on the true operating margin: it omits the thin orbital opex slice (comms / ground-station, mission operations, insurance) and is before financing and tax. The *venture* still pays financing cost on a large front-loaded CAPEX raise and pays tax, both below the operating line. So R = 1.5 should be presented as *"revenue is set so the node earns roughly a 33% margin over its amortized build-and-launch cost, which for a near-zero-opex orbital node is close to an OPERATING margin, comparable to a mature hyperscaler cloud and far above the neoclouds"*, and never as *"the venture earns a 33% bottom-line profit."* The net, after-financing-and-tax return is lower and is the job of the company valuation model (`valuation/VALUATION_MODEL.md`), where `review_economist.md` Findings 1-2 (charge full straight-line depreciation; net financing and tax) directly govern how much of the 33% survives to the investor. R is sound; what it is a margin *of* (an operating-equivalent, not a net return) must stay precise.

## 6. Depreciation period: our 5-year service life vs operators' 6-year (and the margin effect)

A comparability point the founder raised, and a decisive one for reading R honestly. R's denominator is **amortized CAPEX = build-and-launch cost ÷ service life.** The service life we assume is therefore *the* lever that sets annual depreciation, and it must be compared to the depreciation lives the operators actually book. **The operators book LONGER lives than our model, several of them extended their lives recently, and a longer life mechanically lowers annual depreciation and flatters reported margins. Our 5-year service life is shorter and therefore more conservative: it does not flatter the orbital margin, and if anything understates it relative to how operators book depreciation.**

**The mechanism, stated plainly [FACT].** Depreciation is (cost − salvage) ÷ useful life. Stretching the assumed useful life spreads the same cost over more years, so the *annual* depreciation charge falls, cost of revenue falls, and reported gross and operating margins rise, with no change in the underlying economics. The difference between a six-year and a five-year life "effectively lowers depreciation expense by hundreds of millions of dollars annually" and thereby inflates reported earnings ([CNBC, via Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/michael-burrys-latest-warning-could-174332312.html); [Hudson Labs: accounting policy changes boost tech earnings](https://www.hudson-labs.com/post/accounting-policy-changes-boost-tech-earnings)). Conversely, a *shorter* assumed life raises the annual charge and *depresses* reported margin. This is why the service life in R's denominator is not a neutral modeling choice: it directly sets how high the margin can look.

**What the major operators actually use [FACT].** Server/networking useful lives have been ratcheted up across the industry, from ~3 years a decade ago to 5-6 years now, with a partial reversal at Amazon in 2025 once AI obsolescence bit. All figures cross-checked to 2+ independent sources:

**The 2024-2026 revisions, including the reversal [FACT].** The trend was uniformly upward (4 → 5 → 6 years) until the AI build-out forced a reconsideration. **Amazon reversed** from 6 back to 5 years effective Jan 1 2025, explicitly citing "the increased pace of technology development, particularly in the area of artificial intelligence and machine learning" ([deepquarry](https://deepquarry.substack.com/p/amazon-revises-server-lifespan-amid), [Behind the Balance Sheet](https://behindthebalancesheet.substack.com/p/amazons-ai-reality-check)). Meanwhile **Oracle went the other way in FY2025**, extending 5 → 6, and **Meta** extended to 5.5 in early 2025, so the field now spans 5 (Amazon) to 6 (Microsoft, Google, Oracle, CoreWeave). The disagreement is itself the signal: short-seller Michael Burry argued the true useful life of AI server equipment is closer to **1-3 years** and estimated the five largest hyperscalers would **understate depreciation by a cumulative ~$176B over 2026-2028** by booking 5-6 year lives ([CNBC, via Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/michael-burrys-latest-warning-could-174332312.html), [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2025/11/11/michael-burrys-latest-warning-could-be-bad-news-fo/)). Whether or not Burry is right, the direction of the bias is not in dispute: a longer assumed life lowers depreciation and flatters reported margins, and most operators sit at the long (6-year) end.

**Why our 5-year life is conservative AND physically appropriate [INFERENCE].** Two independent reasons converge on the same number, which is what makes it defensible rather than arbitrary:
1. **It is more conservative than the operators' booking.** At 5 years we charge a higher annual depreciation than a 6-year operator on the same hardware cost. So our 33.3% margin is computed against a *heavier* depreciation charge than Microsoft, Google, Oracle, or CoreWeave use. **Our 5-year assumption does not flatter the orbital margin; if anything it understates it relative to how the operators book depreciation.** If we re-stated the orbital node on a 6-year life like the hyperscalers, the implied margin would *rise*, not fall. The conservatism is baked into the denominator.
2. **It is physically appropriate, where the operators' stretch is partly an accounting choice.** A ground operator can leave a depreciated server racked and running, or swap a board, so stretching the booked life to 6 years is partly a margin-optimizing accounting decision (and, per Amazon's reversal and Burry's critique, arguably an aggressive one in the AI era). **An orbital node cannot be refreshed in place**: there is no hands-on upgrade, no board swap, no incremental capacity add once it is on orbit, so its economic life really is bounded by the silicon's competitive life (~2-3 years, see `premium_value_case.md` §8) and the platform's service life. A short, 5-year life is therefore the *honest* life for an un-upgradeable asset, not a pessimistic assumption. This is exactly the discipline `review_economist.md` **Finding 1** demands: charge the **full straight-line depreciation on deployed capital** over the service life, not an attrition-weighted fraction of a live fleet. Our 5-year service life is that full-charge, short-life treatment, the opposite of the life-stretching that flatters ground operators' margins.

**Net read-through [INFERENCE].** The depreciation-period comparison reinforces the operating-margin verdict rather than threatening it. We compute orbit's ~33% operating-equivalent margin against a *more* aggressive (shorter, heavier) depreciation schedule than the operators we compare it to. So when we say the orbital 33% is comparable to a hyperscaler's 33-45% operating margin, the comparison is conservative on the depreciation axis too: the hyperscaler's margin is partly inflated by a 6-year life and recent life extensions, whereas ours is depressed by a 5-year life. The orbital margin is the more honest of the two.

## Open Questions

1. **CAPEX-basis vs cost-of-revenue-basis mismatch.** R is defined over *amortized CAPEX* (build + launch ÷ service life). Operator gross margins are defined over *cost of revenue* (which for a terrestrial operator includes power, lease, and labor on top of depreciation). For the orbital node these two cost bases nearly coincide (opex ≈ 0), but the mapping of R to operator gross margins is therefore approximate, not exact. A cleaner reconciliation would express both on an identical cost base.
2. **Does the orbital near-zero-opex assumption hold? (now addressed in the BLUF and §1, but the line item is still owed.)** The claim that R ≈ an *operating* margin for orbit, which is the document's central point, depends on orbital opex being a thin slice rather than a thick wedge. Comms / ground-station cost, mission operations staff, downlink/bandwidth, and insurance are real opex lines that the model treats as minimal, and §1 now states plainly that they make the orbital 33% an *upper bound* on the true operating margin. The residual open work is to *quantify* that slice as a dedicated orbital-opex line item, so the upper bound can be tightened to a point estimate. The qualitative conclusion (the orbital gross-to-operating gap is far smaller than a ground operator's) is settled; the dollar size of the gap is not yet pinned.
3. **Depreciation honesty and period (now addressed in new §6; cross-ref `review_economist.md` Finding 1).** R = 1.5 is a credible operating-equivalent margin only if the amortized-CAPEX denominator charges the *full* deployed-capital depreciation (straight-line over service life), not an attrition-weighted fraction. §6 now establishes that our **5-year** service life is *shorter* than the 6-year lives most operators book (and shorter than several recent life *extensions*), so our annual depreciation charge is heavier and the orbital margin is, if anything, understated relative to operator accounting. The verdict assumes the model in fact charges this full 5-year straight-line depreciation per `review_economist.md` Finding 1; the residual open item is to confirm the live model wires that charge correctly (the service-life-cliff fix in recent commits is the relevant work).
4. **Is 33.3% gross enough to clear the obsolescence hurdle?** `premium_value_case.md` §8 flags that an un-upgradeable orbital node must earn back its full cost inside the silicon's ~2-3 year competitive life. The mapping of the high band edge is the most time-sensitive figure here.
6. **Reserved-vs-on-demand mix in the revenue-to-cost multiple.** The NextPlatform ~3.5× multiple uses a 50/30/20 on-demand/1yr/3yr blend at AWS *list* pricing. Realized neocloud pricing (and take-or-pay contract discounts) would lower the multiple materially. The 3.5× should be read as a list-price ceiling, not a realized owner-operator multiple.
