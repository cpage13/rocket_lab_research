## 1. The Company

Rocket Lab was founded in **June 2006** by **Peter Beck**, a self-taught New Zealand aerospace engineer, after a US trip convinced him there was a market for low-cost small-rocket launch. In 2009 it became the first private company in the Southern Hemisphere to reach space (Ātea-1 sounding rocket). The company re-domiciled to the United States in 2013 (initially Huntington Beach, CA) and moved its headquarters to **Long Beach, California in 2020**.

Beck remains **founder, President and CEO** and is the company's defining strategic voice. Rocket Lab went public in **August 2021** via SPAC merger and trades on NASDAQ as **RKLB**.

The company has scaled dramatically. Headcount is roughly **2,600 employees** (May 2026), with the majority US-based and additional sites in New Zealand, Maryland, Colorado, New Mexico (Albuquerque), Virginia, and Toronto, plus Munich, Germany following the Mynaric acquisition.

**Scale check:** Market cap of roughly **$72–73 billion** (13 May 2026) is striking against $602M of FY2025 revenue — the valuation is forward-looking, pricing in Neutron, Space Systems growth, and defense backlog. The figure is volatile; treat any single-day number as a snapshot. (Note: the company's market cap was around $1.8B in early 2024, so 2024–2026 saw an extraordinary re-rating.)

### Two operating segments

Rocket Lab reports along two segments:

1. **Launch Services** — Electron (operational small-lift), HASTE (suborbital hypersonic test variant of Electron), and Neutron (medium-lift, in development). Q1 2026 revenue: **$63.7M**.
2. **Space Systems** — spacecraft buses/platforms, spacecraft components and subsystems, on-orbit operations, and (newly) sensor payloads and laser communications terminals. Q1 2026 revenue: **$136.7M** — now the larger segment.

The center of gravity has shifted: Space Systems is the bigger revenue line and, per company commentary, **~42% of total backlog** in Q1 2026 (up from ~26% in Q4 2025). Government/defense work grew to ~49% of backlog.

## 3. Electron (brief — see `electron/electron_specs.md`)

Electron is Rocket Lab's operational **small-lift orbital rocket**: ~18 m tall, 1.2 m diameter, ~13,000 kg, two stages, carbon-composite, powered by 3D-printed electric-pump-fed **Rutherford** engines. Payload ~**300 kg to LEO** (~200 kg typical to 500 km SSO). It has flown ~**80+ missions since 2017** and delivered **21 launches in 2025 at 100% mission success**. Launch sites: **Launch Complex 1, Māhia, New Zealand** (two pads) and **Launch Complex 2, Wallops Island, Virginia**. Electron is the most-flown US small launcher and second only to SpaceX among Western providers by 2025 launch count. Rocket Lab is pursuing partial reusability (ocean recovery of the first stage; first reflight in progress).

## 4. Space Systems Segment

This is the strategically important segment for a space-data-center thesis: Rocket Lab can both **launch and build** the spacecraft.

### Satellite platforms / buses
- **Photon** — a satellite bus derived from Electron's kick stage; customizable for LEO payload hosting, lunar, and interplanetary missions. Heritage missions include NASA's CAPSTONE lunar mission and a custom Photon for Varda Space Industries. Photon variants continue flying in 2026 (e.g., LOXSAT cryogenic-fluid demo with Eta Space/NASA; a Viasat data-relay demo).
- **Flatellite** — announced **February 2025**: a flat, **stackable, high-power, mass-manufacturable** satellite designed for **large constellations**, engineered for tight integration with Neutron (stacks of up to ~16 per launch; estimated mass slightly over ~800 kg — unofficial). Flatellite is the basis of Rocket Lab's own potential constellation ambitions and is being produced for the SDA contract. Backlog reported to exceed 40 spacecraft.
- Earlier/heritage buses: **Lightning** and **Explorer** classes.

### Components and subsystems (largely in-house)
Solar arrays (STARRAY family / SolAero space-grade solar cells), reaction wheels and star trackers (Sinclair Interplanetary heritage), propulsion, separation systems, Frontier deep-space radios, flight and ground software, composite structures, and launch dispensers. Components/subsystems are believed to carry the highest gross margins in the portfolio.

### Recent acquisitions deepening vertical integration
- **Geost** — ~$275M, announced May 2025: EO/IR sensor payloads for missile warning, tracking, space domain awareness. Moves Rocket Lab into the **payload** business.
- **Mynaric AG** (Munich) — completed **April 2026**: CONDOR-class **laser optical communications terminals** (inter-satellite links). Directly relevant to high-bandwidth orbital networking.
- **Motiv Space Systems** (Pasadena) — agreement announced May 2026, expected to close Q2 2026: Mars-proven **robotics**, precision mechanisms, solar array drive assemblies (SADAs).
- Plus smaller bolt-ons (e.g., machining/optics capacity).

The cumulative effect: Rocket Lab now manufactures laser terminals, thrusters, optics, solar power, structures, and robotic mechanisms in-house — closing most gaps between "launch company" and "full-stack space company."

## 5. Neutron — High-Level Status Only

*(Detailed analysis is owned by a separate agent — kept brief here.)*

Neutron is Rocket Lab's **medium-lift, partially reusable** rocket: ~43 m tall, ~7 m diameter, nine in-house **Archimedes** engines on a reusable first stage, carbon-composite structures, with a distinctive "Hungry Hippo" fairing that retains the second stage. Payload class: up to ~13,000 kg to LEO with downrange booster landing (up to ~15,000 kg expended; ~8,500 kg with return-to-launch-site).

**Status (May 2026):** Pre-flight. A Stage-1 propellant tank failed a hydrostatic pressure test in January 2026, pushing the maiden flight to **Q4 2026**. The failed tank is being replaced with an automated-fiber-placement unit to eliminate the hand-lay defect class. FAA launch permits filed for a July 1–Dec 31, 2026 window. Archimedes engine qualification, second stage, and reusable fairing all cleared additional milestones in Q1 2026. Five commercial Neutron bookings signed in Q1 2026.

Neutron matters to the data-center thesis because it is the vehicle sized to deploy stacked Flatellite constellations and larger orbital infrastructure.

## 6. Strategic Read

**Is Rocket Lab positioning toward end-to-end space services?** Yes — explicitly and aggressively.

- Peter Beck has publicly modeled Rocket Lab on SpaceX's trajectory: start with launch, add spacecraft, build components in-house, become an end-to-end space infrastructure company. The 2025–2026 acquisition spree (Geost, Mynaric, Motiv) is the execution of that thesis.
- The company describes itself as an **"end-to-end space company"** spanning launch, spacecraft manufacture, components, and on-orbit management/operations.
- The **$816M SDA constellation prime contract** demonstrates Rocket Lab can win and deliver as a satellite prime — build *and* operate, not just launch.
- **Flatellite + Neutron** is a deliberate launch-plus-build constellation system; Rocket Lab is positioned to deploy constellations (potentially its own) rapidly and cheaply.

**Direct relevance — space-based compute / data centers:** On **26 February 2026**, Rocket Lab announced **advanced silicon solar arrays explicitly designed to power gigawatt-scale space-based data centers** spanning kilometers in orbit. Rocket Lab's framing: terrestrial data centers are power-, land-, and water-constrained, while orbit offers abundant volume, a cold environment, and limitless solar energy; **power is the gating factor** for orbital compute scalability, and Rocket Lab's mass-manufacturable solar arrays target low cost-per-watt at industrial scale.

Note the positioning: in that announcement Rocket Lab cast itself as a **supplier of power infrastructure** to the space-based data center industry, not (yet) as an operator of orbital data centers. This is an important nuance for the feasibility study — Rocket Lab is publicly signaling it wants to enable/equip orbital data centers and has the launch + bus + power + comms + robotics stack to do so, but has not declared itself an orbital data-center operator.

**Bottom line:** Rocket Lab is a vertically integrating, fast-growing, well-capitalized space company that can launch, build, power, link, and operate spacecraft. It has explicitly named space-based data centers as a target market for its solar-array business. For a project evaluating orbital AI-inference data centers on Neutron, Rocket Lab is unusually well-aligned as a potential end-to-end partner.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

- **Market cap** is highly volatile; the ~$72–73B figure is a 13 May 2026 snapshot and the valuation is far ahead of current revenue. Verify against a live quote before use.
- **Employee count** (~2,600) is approximate and moves with each acquisition close (Motiv, Geost integration).
- **Space Systems FY revenue split** — some third-party estimates put Space Systems at 58–65% of FY2025 revenue; the cleanest hard figure here is the Q1 2026 segment split ($136.7M / $63.7M). Treat full-year segment percentages as estimates.
- **Flatellite specs** — mass (~800 kg), power output, and per-launch stacking (~16) are largely unofficial / render-derived. Rocket Lab has not published a full Flatellite datasheet.
- **Neutron schedule** — Q4 2026 maiden flight is the current target after the Jan 2026 tank failure; first-flight slips are common in this industry.
- **Space-based data center role** — Rocket Lab has announced solar arrays *for* orbital data centers but has made no public commitment to *operate* orbital data centers or host third-party compute payloads. Whether it would build/operate an AI-inference data center (vs. supply components) is an open question to confirm directly.
- Q1 2026 figures are the latest available as of this writing (reported 7 May 2026); FY2026 will not be known until early 2027.
