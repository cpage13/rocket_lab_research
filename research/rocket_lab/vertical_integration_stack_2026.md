# Rocket Lab's Vertical-Integration Stack: How Much of a Data-Center Node Does It Already Make In-House?

**Research date:** 2026-06-01
**Purpose:** A data-center "node" is essentially a satellite (bus + solar + thermal/radiators + comms + avionics + propulsion) wrapped around GPUs. This doc enumerates, with sources, which of those subsystems Rocket Lab actually designs, manufactures, and owns today (organically or by acquisition), how complete that vertical integration is, and what the margin-capture implication is for the cost of a node. It is the "structural case" pillar for Rocket Lab's spacecraft hardware integration.
**Scope note:** This is a vertical-integration / margin-capture lens. The companion doc [`rocket_lab/space_hardware_capabilities.md`](space_hardware_capabilities.md) does the deeper per-subsystem capability dive (efficiency numbers, Flatellite power, the node-scale radiator/thermal scale-up question). This doc does NOT re-derive those; it reasons about ownership and supplier-margin capture and cross-references that doc for capability depth.

## BLUF (bottom line up front)

**Rocket Lab already makes, in-house, almost every subsystem of a satellite bus that an orbital data-center node needs, and it has been deliberately buying or building the long-lead pieces specifically to stop paying supplier margin on them.** Confirmed in-house, flying today: space-grade solar cells and full arrays (SolAero, CLOSED Jan 2022) [FACT]; reaction wheels and star trackers (Sinclair Interplanetary, CLOSED Apr 2020) [FACT]; separation systems / the Motorized Lightband (Planetary Systems Corp, CLOSED Dec 2021) [FACT]; software-defined radios (Frontier, organic) [FACT]; chemical propulsion (Curie / HyperCurie, organic) [FACT]; flight and ground software (MAX / GNC via Advanced Solutions Inc, CLOSED Oct 2021, on top of organic avionics) [FACT]; composite structures and tanks plus in-house precision machining (Precision Components Ltd, CLOSED Feb 2026), and satellite buses (Photon, and the high-power stackable Flatellite) [FACT]; spacecraft-scale power management and thermal-control subsystems flown on the Photon bus [FACT]; EO/IR plus optical payload electronics (Geost, CLOSED Aug 2025) [FACT]; and mechanisms, actuators, drive electronics and solar-array drive assemblies (Motiv Space Systems / "Rocket Lab Robotics", CLOSED 26 May 2026) [FACT].

**The acquisitions called out in the brief are now confirmed and dated, with three corrections of status / framing:**
- **Laser inter-satellite communications: Mynaric AG. CLOSED 14 April 2026 for ~$155.3M** (a nominal cash payment plus 2,277,002 RKLB shares; Stock Purchase Agreement dated 25 Sept 2025). Mynaric makes the CONDOR optical communications terminals. [FACT]
- **Electric propulsion is NOT an acquisition. The capability the brief calls "Gauss" is real, but it is ORGANIC: Gauss is Rocket Lab's own in-house-designed and -built Hall-effect electric-propulsion system, unveiled 14 April 2026, with a production line sized for >200 thrusters/year. Rocket Lab's chief engineer explicitly said they considered acquiring an existing EP company and instead chose to build it in-house.** [FACT] (The "Geost" acquisition, which sounds adjacent, is a separate deal and is EO/IR sensor payloads, not propulsion. [FACT])
- **The "robotic arms / actuators / electronic-components" company is Motiv Space Systems, and it has now CLOSED.** A reviewer flagged a recently closed acquisition of a space robotic-arm / actuator / drive-electronics maker, possibly "N"-named. A full search (Wikipedia acquisition list, SEC 8-Ks, SpaceNews, GlobeNewswire, the Q1-2026 trade roundups) finds NO "N"-named Rocket Lab acquisition. The deal that matches the description exactly, robotic arms, actuators, drive electronics, motor controllers, gimbals, filter wheels and SADAs, is **Motiv Space Systems (Pasadena, CA), rebranded "Rocket Lab Robotics", which CLOSED on 26 May 2026** (Equity Purchase Agreement 6 May 2026; ~$60M total, $40M cash upfront plus a $20M contingent equity earnout). The prior version of this doc listed Motiv as PENDING; that is now corrected to CLOSED. [FACT]

**On thermal and power for a compute node: this is a question of SCALE, not capability or intent.** Rocket Lab designs and builds its own satellites and makes its own solar cells and arrays (SolAero), and the Photon bus already carries Rocket Lab's in-house "power management, thermal control and attitude control" subsystems. So spacecraft-scale thermal and power management are firmly in-house today. [FACT] What does NOT yet exist publicly is a LARGE-SCALE deployable radiator product or a hundreds-of-kW-to-MW PMAD product sized for a compute payload. But a node's radiator is too large a cost line, and too tightly coupled to the solar and thermal system Rocket Lab already owns, to be bought on the open market; the structural expectation is that Rocket Lab brings node-scale radiators and high-power PMAD in-house, organically or by acquisition, exactly as it has done serially across every other long-lead subsystem, NOT that it outsources them. The open item is scale-up, not a missing competency. [INFERENCE]

**Margin-capture takeaway (qualitative, no invented percentage):** because a data-center node is mostly satellite-bus hardware, and Rocket Lab makes all of that hardware in-house at spacecraft scale, the supplier margin that an outside integrator would pay on solar, wheels, trackers, radios, separation, propulsion, structures, mechanisms/SADAs, thermal and power management, and now laser terminals is captured internally. The same dollar buys more node, or the same node costs less, than a conservative outsider build that buys those subsystems on the merchant market. Rocket Lab states this benefit directly: when it bids constellation contracts it does not "lose margins to external suppliers," and CEO Peter Beck frames vertical integration as "not a religion ... not even a strategy. It's just a necessity." [FACT] The base financial model does not price this advantage; it is a structural reason the real build is cheaper than the modeled one. [INFERENCE]

## 1. The node-to-satellite mapping (what we are checking ownership of)

An orbital data-center node, stripped to subsystems, is a satellite carrying GPUs. The bus subsystems it needs are the same ones any high-power LEO satellite needs:

| Node subsystem | Why the node needs it |
|---|---|
| Satellite bus / platform | The structural and functional chassis the GPUs ride in |
| Solar (cells + arrays) | Primary power for the compute payload |
| Thermal / radiators | Reject the GPUs' waste heat (the hard one) |
| Comms (RF + optical/laser) | Move data to ground and node-to-node |
| Avionics / flight computer / flight software | Command, control, fault management |
| Attitude control (reaction wheels, star trackers, sun sensors) | Point the arrays, radiators, and antennas |
| Propulsion (chemical + electric) | Orbit insertion, station-keeping, end-of-life deorbit |
| Structures / composites / tanks | Hold it together; survive launch loads |
| Separation system | Release the node(s) from the launch vehicle |
| Batteries / power storage | Power through eclipse |
| Power management / distribution (PMAD/PCDU) | Route hundreds of kW from arrays to payload |
| Mechanisms (SADAs, gimbals, actuators) | Articulate arrays/antennas; deploy structures |

The rest of this doc states, subsystem by subsystem, whether Rocket Lab owns the capability, organically or by acquisition, and whether the relevant deal is CLOSED or PENDING.

### 2.1 What this table says at a glance

Of the twelve bus subsystems in the node-to-satellite mapping (Section 1), Rocket Lab **owns all twelve at spacecraft scale today** (solar; reaction wheels; star trackers; sun sensors; separation; RF; laser comms; chemical and electric propulsion; bus; flight/ground software/avionics; structures/tanks/precision machining/batteries; mechanisms/SADAs/actuators via Motiv, now CLOSED; and spacecraft-scale thermal control and power management on the Photon bus). The compute payload itself is intentionally out of scope. **There is no bus subsystem the node needs that Rocket Lab does not already build at spacecraft scale.** The two genuinely open items are not missing capabilities but missing SCALE: a large deployable radiator and a hundreds-of-kW-to-MW PMAD sized for a compute payload are not yet published products, and (per the rows above) the structural expectation is that Rocket Lab brings those in-house rather than outsourcing them. That is an unusually complete in-house bus stack. [INFERENCE, from the FACT rows above]

### 3.2 Electric propulsion = "Gauss", and it is ORGANIC, not an acquisition [FACT]
The brief referenced an electric-propulsion capability "as 'Gauss'" and asked to confirm the real company / deal status. The finding is a meaningful correction:

- **Gauss is real, and it is in-house, not acquired.** "Rocket Lab's in-house designed and manufactured electric propulsion system, named Gauss," was **unveiled 14 April 2026** and "features a Hall Thruster, Power Processing Unit and a Propellant Management Assembly," with a line "designed to produce more than 200 thrusters per year." [FACT] ([RL Gauss PR](https://rocketlabcorp.com/updates/rocket-lab-unveils-new-electric-propulsion-satellite-thruster-to-meet-constellation-demand/); [GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/04/14/3273398/0/en/Rocket-Lab-Unveils-New-Electric-Propulsion-Satellite-Thruster-to-Meet-Constellation-Demand.html))
- **Build-vs-buy was an explicit choice.** Rocket Lab's chief engineer said the company often acquires high-performing tech that has struggled to scale, and "We explored this as an option for Gauss but ultimately determined we could build the best, high-performance product in house" and scale it themselves. [FACT] ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/04/14/3273398/0/en/Rocket-Lab-Unveils-New-Electric-Propulsion-Satellite-Thruster-to-Meet-Constellation-Demand.html); [StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/RKLB/rocket-lab-unveils-new-electric-propulsion-satellite-thruster-to-8jbt3hru3nr3.html))
- **Performance framing:** designed for higher specific impulse than chemical propulsion, letting a spacecraft carry less propellant for station-keeping and long-duration missions. No certified Isp / power datasheet number was published as of this date. [FACT for the qualitative claim; the absence of a hard number is noted]
- **Do not confuse "Gauss" (EP, organic) with "Geost" (EO/IR payloads, acquired).** Geost (CLOSED Aug 2025, $275M) is electro-optical/infrared sensor payloads, a different product line entirely; it is not propulsion. [FACT] ([SpaceNews Geost](https://spacenews.com/rocket-lab-to-acquire-satellite-payload-manufacturer-geost-for-275-million/))
- **Why it matters for a node:** station-keeping and end-of-life deorbit for a large LEO compute node are EP-favorable; owning the thruster captures EP supplier margin and de-risks a notoriously supply-constrained component, which is exactly the public rationale Rocket Lab gave. See the project's EP station-keeping analysis in [`node_design/electric_propulsion_stationkeeping_5v7yr.md`](../node_design/electric_propulsion_stationkeeping_5v7yr.md). [INFERENCE]

## 4. How complete is the vertical integration?

**Complete at spacecraft scale across every node bus subsystem; the only open items are scaling two subsystems up to compute-node size.** Counting the node-to-satellite mapping:

- **Owned and flying (organic or CLOSED acquisition):** solar (cells through arrays), reaction wheels, star trackers, sun sensors, separation systems, RF radios, laser comms, chemical propulsion, electric propulsion, satellite bus, flight/ground software and GNC (ASI/MAX), avionics, composites/tanks and precision machining, batteries (bus-scale), spacecraft-scale thermal control and power management (Photon bus), EO/IR payload electronics, and now mechanisms/SADAs/actuators/drive electronics (Motiv, CLOSED 26 May 2026). That is the entire conventional small/medium-satellite bus, including its thermal and power subsystems.
- **Open at node scale (not missing capability, missing scale):** a LARGE deployable thermal radiator and a hundreds-of-kW-to-MW PMAD for a compute payload are not yet published Rocket Lab products. But Rocket Lab already does spacecraft-scale thermal control and power management in-house (Photon), and a node's radiator is too large a cost line and too tightly coupled to the solar/thermal system Rocket Lab already owns (SolAero arrays + bus thermal) to be bought on the merchant market. The defensible expectation is in-house scale-up, organically or by acquisition, on the same pattern Rocket Lab has used for every other long-lead subsystem, NOT outsourcing. [INFERENCE]
- **Out of scope by design:** the GPU/server payload itself.

So the honest characterization is: **Rocket Lab is an end-to-end prime for the satellite platform, power, thermal, comms, attitude, propulsion, mechanisms, and launch of a node today, and the only node-specific work remaining is scaling its existing in-house radiator/thermal and power-management capability up to compute-payload power levels, not acquiring a capability it lacks.** This matches the company's own "end-to-end space company" positioning while not overstating it: Beck's framing is "not a religion ... not even a strategy. It's just a necessity," and the integration is reported as spanning "spacecraft manufacture, satellite subsystems, flight software, ground operations, and launch." [FACT] ([SolAero acquire PR](https://rocketlabcorp.com/updates/rocket-lab-to-acquire-solaero-holdings-inc-a-global-leader-in-space-solar-power-products/); [Bloomberg "Tech Disruptors" interview](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/audio/2025-09-23/tech-disruptors-rocket-lab-s-ceo-on-end-to-end-space-solutions))

## 5. The margin-capture implication for a data-center node

This section characterizes the implication **qualitatively**. Per the project rule, no specific supplier-margin percentage is invented here.

**The argument, made precise.** A data-center node is, by mass and by subsystem count, mostly satellite-bus hardware wrapped around GPUs (Section 1). An outside integrator building the same node on the merchant market would buy solar arrays, reaction wheels, star trackers, separation systems, radios, laser terminals, propulsion, structures, mechanisms/SADAs, flight software, and thermal and power-management subsystems from suppliers, and each of those purchases embeds the supplier's gross margin in the node's bill of materials. Rocket Lab makes all of those subsystems in-house at spacecraft scale (Sections 2-3). When Rocket Lab builds a node, the margin an outsider would pay to those suppliers is instead captured internally rather than paid out. The mechanical consequence: **for the same input cost, Rocket Lab fields more node; for the same node, Rocket Lab spends less cash, than the conservative outsider build the base model implicitly assumes.** [INFERENCE]

**Rocket Lab states this benefit in its own words.** Trade coverage of its strategy is explicit that when Rocket Lab "bids for multi-hundred-million-dollar constellation contracts, they won't lose margins to external suppliers, giving them ... pricing power," and that "from star trackers and solar panels to reaction wheels and now laser terminals and thrusters, Rocket Lab is replicating SpaceX's core competency of vertical integration." [FACT] ([CMC Markets / Opto profile](https://www.cmcmarkets.com/en-gb/opto/rocket-lab-manufacturing-space-solutions); [Bloomberg interview](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/audio/2025-09-23/tech-disruptors-rocket-lab-s-ceo-on-end-to-end-space-solutions)) This is corroboration that the margin-capture effect is real and intended, not merely an analyst inference.

**Why this is a structural advantage the base model does not price.** A conservative outsider feasibility model (the kind this study's base case represents) would cost a node's subsystems at merchant prices, because that is the defensible assumption for an arbitrary builder. Rocket Lab is not an arbitrary builder: it is simultaneously the launch provider, the bus manufacturer, and the maker of almost every subsystem. The vertical stack means the node BOM it actually faces is closer to its own internal cost-to-build than to a merchant-priced BOM. Three compounding effects: (1) **supplier-margin capture** on each in-house subsystem; (2) **long-lead-item de-risking**, since the components Beck repeatedly cites as supply-fragile (reaction wheels, star trackers, SADAs, EP thrusters, optical terminals) are exactly the ones Rocket Lab brought in-house, removing both a price markup and a schedule risk; and (3) **volume scaling**, since Rocket Lab is explicitly standing up high-volume lines (200+ Gauss thrusters/year, a dedicated reaction-wheel facility, mass-manufactured Flatellite, silicon arrays for industrial-scale volume) aimed at constellation-scale unit counts, which is the regime an orbital data-center fleet would need. [INFERENCE, anchored to the FACTs in Sections 2-3]

**The honest boundary on the claim.** Vertical integration captures supplier margin; it does not make subsystems free, and it does not by itself retire the engineering work of scaling the node-specific subsystems (the large deployable radiator and the high-power PMAD) up from spacecraft scale to compute-payload scale. That is a scale-up problem on top of capability Rocket Lab already owns in-house, not a missing capability or a subsystem Rocket Lab would have to go buy from a competitor. It also does not, by itself, prove the node is cheaper than a ground data center; the project's ground-comparison work (see SOURCE_INDEX `RLDC-GROUND-ORBIT-RATIO-2036`) is a separate question. The defensible statement is narrow and strong: **relative to a conservative outsider who buys the bus on the merchant market, Rocket Lab's in-house stack lowers the real cost of a node by capturing supplier margin across the entire bus, including thermal and power, and the base model, which does not assume an owner who also makes the bus, does not capture that.** [INFERENCE]

**On putting a number on it.** If a quantified margin-capture figure is ever needed for the model, it must carry an [ESTIMATE] tag and rest on 2+ independent sources for the underlying supplier-margin assumption (e.g. published space-component gross margins or comparable vertically integrated operator margins). This doc deliberately does not assert one. As a single illustrative anchor only, satellite-component and space-hardware gross margins are commonly discussed in the 20-40% range in industry commentary, but that band is not sourced to the standard required for a model input and must not be promoted as a project number without independent corroboration. [ESTIMATE, deliberately not adopted]

## 7. Open questions / uncertainties

- **Large deployable radiators (node scale): a scale-up question, not a capability gap.** Rocket Lab designs and flies its own spacecraft-scale thermal-control subsystem on the Photon bus, and makes its own solar cells/arrays (SolAero), so the thermal and solar systems a radiator couples to are already in-house. What is not yet public is a LARGE deployable-radiator product sized for a compute payload. Because a node's radiator is a major cost line tightly coupled to the in-house solar/thermal system, the structural expectation is in-house build (organic or acquisition), not a merchant-market purchase. The open item is the scale-up program and its timing, not whether Rocket Lab does thermal at all. Confirm if/when a node-scale radiator program is disclosed. [INFERENCE]
- **Sun sensors: now confirmed in-house (bus scale).** Sun sensors are explicitly listed among the in-house subsystems flown on the Photon "Pathstone" demonstrator. Resolved for spacecraft scale; no separate concern remains.
- **Gauss performance datasheet.** No certified specific-impulse, thrust, or power numbers were published as of 2026-06-01. The in-house and >200/yr claims are sourced; the performance numbers are not yet available.
- **Motiv close: RESOLVED, CLOSED 26 May 2026.** The Motiv ("Rocket Lab Robotics") acquisition signed via an Equity Purchase Agreement on 6 May 2026 (announced 7 May) and COMPLETED on 26 May 2026 (~$60M: $40M cash + $20M contingent equity). SADA / gimbal / actuator / drive-electronics capability is now in-house. No "N"-named acquisition exists; Motiv was the referent. [FACT]
- **Data-center-scale PMAD/PCDU: a scale-up question, not a capability gap.** Spacecraft-scale power management is in-house on the Photon bus. Whether Rocket Lab has power-management electronics sized for hundreds-of-kW-to-MW compute loads is not yet a published product. Same framing as radiators: capability and intent are in-house; the open question is scale-up and its timing.
- **Quantified margin capture.** No project-grade, 2+ source figure for the supplier margin captured per subsystem. Any future number needs the [ESTIMATE] tag and independent corroboration; the illustrative 20-40% gross-margin band in Section 5 is explicitly NOT adopted as a model input.
- **Batteries at node scale.** Photon-class lithium-polymer secondary batteries are confirmed for a small/medium bus; whether the same in-house capability scales to a high-power compute node's eclipse-ride-through energy storage is unverified.
