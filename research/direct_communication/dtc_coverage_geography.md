This doc answers four bounded questions that ground a coverage simulation for a low-orbit direct-to-cell service:
1. **Population by latitude:** what fraction of people (and of mobile demand) lie within +/-55 deg, +/-45 deg, +/-35 deg latitude?
2. **The latitude-band insight:** why does covering "US + Europe latitudes" automatically cover the same-latitude bands of South America, Africa, Asia, and Australia?
3. **Inclination choice:** what inclination should a populated-band D2C constellation fly, and why?
4. **The 95% target:** terrestrial mobile covers ~95% of people, not 100% of land. What does targeting 95% of population (instead of 100% of a latitude band) do to the satellite count?

## Summary / Verdict

**Confidence: high** on the geometry (inclination = max ground-track latitude, Earth-rotation longitude fill, the 53-deg industry standard) because each is a sourced, multiply-corroborated orbital-mechanics fact; **medium-high** on the population-by-latitude band fractions because no public source publishes the exact cumulative-fraction curve, so the +/-55 / +/-45 / +/-35 numbers are reconstructed from multi-sourced anchors (shown with arithmetic) rather than quoted whole.

1. **About 95% of the world's people live within +/-55 deg latitude. The working assumption is correct, and if anything slightly conservative.** The reconstructed cumulative fractions are ~96-97% within +/-55 deg, ~92-95% within +/-45 deg, and ~78-83% within +/-35 deg. The distribution is heavily Northern-Hemisphere-skewed (~87-90% of people live north of the equator), so each +/-band is dominated by its Northern half; the high-latitude tails of both hemispheres hold almost nobody (north of 55N is only ~3-4% of the world; south of 55S is a rounding error). [ESTIMATE for the band fractions, reconstructed; FACT for the anchors] An independent cross-check from the demand side: the ITU reports that ~95% of the world population already lives inside the footprint of a terrestrial mobile-broadband network, so "95% of people" is both the population-geography answer AND the served-mobile-demand answer. [FACT, ITU]

2. **An inclined constellation at inclination i covers ALL longitudes within roughly +/-i latitude, because the Earth rotates underneath the fixed orbit planes.** A satellite's ground track oscillates between +i and -i latitude (it never crosses a latitude higher than its inclination), and on each ~90-min pass the Earth has turned ~22.5 deg eastward under the plane, so successive passes walk westward and, over a day, the constellation sweeps every longitude inside the band. **The consequence is the load-bearing geography insight: a constellation sized for "US + Europe" latitudes is not a US+Europe constellation at all, it is a global MID-LATITUDE-BAND constellation that simultaneously covers the same-latitude slices of South America, Africa, South and East Asia, and Australia for free.** You do not pay extra to cover Sao Paulo, Johannesburg, Mumbai, Shanghai, or Sydney once you have paid to cover the US+Europe latitude band; they sit in the same band. [FACT, orbital mechanics, multi-source]

3. **Fly the primary shell at ~53 deg inclination. This is the validated industry standard for serving the populated mid-latitudes.** Both leading direct-to-cell systems (Starlink Direct-to-Cell and AST SpaceMobile BlueBird) fly 53.0 deg as their primary inclination; Globalstar (the original Apple Emergency SOS partner) flies 52 deg; Amazon's Leo/Kuiper shells are 33/42/51.9 deg; Telesat Lightspeed's capacity layer is 50.9 deg. A 53-deg constellation puts its densest, dwell-weighted coverage right in the ~45-55 deg band (CONUS, the contiguous populated US, the bulk of populated Europe) and reaches into the low 60s with a wide footprint, while never wasting satellite-time over the empty poles, the inefficiency that a polar (~90 deg, Iridium/OneWeb-style) design pays. The tradeoff: 53 deg does NOT serve the Nordics (Oslo/Stockholm ~59-60N, populated to ~71N), northern Canada, or Alaska (~61-65N); the proven answer is to ADD a higher-inclination shell (70 deg, or ~97.6 deg SSO) rather than raise the base inclination. [DERIVED recommendation; the inclination values are FACT, multi-source]

4. **Targeting 95% of population instead of 100% of the latitude band makes the count QUALITATIVELY cheaper, because the last few percent are the geometrically and economically hardest edges.** Terrestrial operators already stop at ~95% of population for a reason: ITU data shows mobile reaches ~95% of people but only ~80-87% of landmass (the UK covers 99% of premises but only 80-87% of its land), because the remaining population is rural, remote, off-grid, low-density, and in developing regions, the most expensive to reach per user. The same shape holds in a constellation: going from "cover the populated band" to "cover 100% of the band including its empty high-latitude edges and the last sparse percent of people" is precisely the move that adds polar shells and multi-fold redundancy, and the constellation-design literature confirms the 95%-to-99%+ jump costs disproportionately. A 95%-of-population D2C target is therefore a single inclined ~53-deg band sized to the SOC floor (the few-hundred-satellite US+Europe-band number, COMM-215/216); chasing the last few percent is what would push toward the polar-shell additions and the multi-fold count multipliers. [DERIVED; the terrestrial 95%/land split and the cost-curve shape are FACT, multi-source]

**Numbers to treat with care (flagged in the claims ledger):** the +/-55 / +/-45 / +/-35 band fractions are reconstructed estimates, not direct quotes (no public source publishes the exact cumulative curve); the +/-35 figure is the softest because it slices through the densest 30-40N belt; the AST 53-deg primary inclination is corroborated (flown orbit plus FCC filing) but the exact SSO-shell figure is single-source; and the satellite-count consequences here are GEOMETRY/target framing, not buildable counts (the buildable floor lives in [`leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md`](leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md)).

## 1. Population by latitude: ~95% within +/-55 deg

### 1.1 The anchors (sourced facts)

### 1.2 The band fractions (reconstructed, arithmetic shown)

No public page publishes "% within +/-55 / +/-45 / +/-35." These three are reconstructed from the sourced anchors plus the WorldPop 2020 per-degree figures. They are computed estimates, not quotes.

Building blocks (sourced): 50% of the world is in 20N-40N; 50% is north of 27N (so ~37-40% sits in 0-27N once the ~12% Southern Hemisphere is removed); Northern Hemisphere ~88%, Southern ~12%; north of 50N ~2-3%.

- **Within +/-35 deg.** Northern side (0-35N): the 20-40N band (50%) sits mostly below 35N (the densest degree-bands are 22-31N), plus the entire heavy 0-20N tropics on top, giving ~62-66%. Southern side (0-35S): nearly the ENTIRE Southern Hemisphere is inside 35S (Brazil ends ~34S, South Africa ~34S, Sydney 34S), giving ~11-13%. **Total ~78-83%.**
- **Within +/-45 deg.** Add the 35N-45N slice (US Midwest/Northeast, Mediterranean Europe to ~45N, northern China/Korea/Japan to ~45N): ~+12-14%. Add the tiny 35S-45S slice (southern Australia, central Chile/Argentina, most of NZ): ~+1%. **Total ~92-95%.**
- **Within +/-55 deg.** Add 45N-55N, the last big chunk (heavily populated Europe: London 51.5N, the German/Polish/Benelux core ~50-52N, Moscow 55.7N at the edge): ~+10-12%, but most of that European mass is below ~52-53N. North of 55N is only ~3-4% of the world (Nordics, St. Petersburg/N. Russia, Scotland, N. Canada). 45S-55S adds ~0.1%. **Total ~96-97%.**

**This confirms the working assumption: ~95% of people within +/-55 deg is correct, with the true value likely ~96-97%, marginally higher.** For a model, round central values of +/-55 = 96%, +/-45 = 93%, +/-35 = 80% (with a +/-3-point band on the +/-35 figure) are defensible.

### 1.3 The demand-side cross-check (ITU): 95% is also the served-mobile number

A second, independent line lands on the same 95%. The ITU's Facts and Figures reports that **~95% of the world population lives within the footprint of a mobile-broadband (3G-or-above) network** (90% for 4G), leaving a ~5% coverage gap, and that **coverage is measured by population, not by land** ([ITU Facts and Figures 2023, Mobile network coverage](https://www.itu.int/itu-d/reports/statistics/2023/10/10/ff23-mobile-network-coverage/); restated [ITU 2022](https://www.itu.int/itu-d/reports/statistics/2022/11/24/ff22-mobile-network-coverage/)). So the +/-55-deg latitude band (~95-97% of people by geography) and the terrestrially-served mobile base (~95% of people by network footprint) are the SAME population, reached from two directions. A D2C constellation sized to the populated mid-latitude band is sized to essentially the entire human mobile-demand base. [FACT, ITU]

### 1.5 Mobile demand is even MORE mid-latitude-concentrated than raw population

Weighting by mobile subscribers pulls the distribution tighter toward 0-45N, not looser. China (~1.81 B subscriptions) and India (~1.14 B) dominate the global subscriber base, and both populations sit entirely within +/-35 to +/-45 ([TheGlobalEconomy / Statista 2024](https://www.theglobaleconomy.com/rankings/mobile_phone_subscribers/)). Asia-Pacific holds the largest share of mobile and ~41% of the 5G market ([GSMA Mobile Economy Asia Pacific 2024](https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-economy/asiapacific-2024/)). The growth frontier (the ~43% of the world not yet on mobile internet) is concentrated in South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, both squarely inside +/-35 ([GSMA](https://www.gsma.com/newsroom/press-release/number-mobile-subscribers-worldwide-hits-5-billion/)). The only "high-latitude, rich, fully connected" populations (Nordics, northern Canada) are a rounding error in absolute subscriber terms. **Net: a constellation covering +/-55 deg captures essentially the entire human mobile-demand base; +/-45 still captures ~90%+ of subscribers; +/-35 captures most of the growth market but cuts off the dense 35-45N belt (most of Europe, northern China/Korea/Japan, the northern US).** [SECONDARY, lighter sourcing]

## 2. The latitude-band insight: covering "US + Europe" means covering a global mid-latitude band

This is the geometry that makes the whole D2C coverage story cheaper than it first appears.

### 2.1 The orbital-mechanics reason (two facts)

**Fact A: a satellite never crosses a latitude higher than its inclination.** For a circular orbit, the sub-satellite ground track oscillates between +i and -i latitude; the maximum latitude reached equals the orbital inclination ([Orbital Radar Academy, Inclination and Ground Tracks](https://orbitalradar.com/academy/orbit-basics/ob02-inclination-ground-tracks): "For a circular orbit, the satellite never passes over a latitude higher than its inclination"; corroborated by [Wikipedia, Satellite ground track](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_ground_track): "the geographic latitudes covered by the ground track will range from -i to i"; [pressbooks Orbital Mechanics Ch. 9](https://oer.pressbooks.pub/lynnanegeorge/chapter/chapter-9-ground-tracks/)). [FACT, multi-source]

**Fact B: the Earth rotates eastward under the fixed orbit plane, so successive passes fill in all longitudes within the band.** "Because Earth rotates beneath a fixed orbital plane, the track shifts westward on each pass" ([Orbital Radar Academy](https://orbitalradar.com/academy/orbit-basics/ob02-inclination-ground-tracks)). At a ~90-95 min LEO period the Earth turns ~22.5-24 deg per orbit, so the ground track walks ~22.5 deg of longitude westward each pass, and over a day a sufficiently-populated set of planes sweeps every longitude inside +/-i. A constellation must therefore "cover all longitudes in the zone of latitude occupied by the target region to allow for Earth rotation relative to the orbital planes," which is exactly the streets-of-coverage construction in [`leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md`](leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md) (planes tiled across the longitude span). [FACT, multi-source; the SOC longitude-tiling is COMM-212]

### 2.2 The consequence: same-latitude bands come free

Put A and B together: an inclined constellation does not cover a region, it covers a LATITUDE BAND (all longitudes, +/-i, plus a footprint margin). The orbit planes do not know or care whether the ground beneath the band is the US, the Atlantic, Europe, the Sahara, or the South Pacific; they cover the band uniformly as the Earth turns. So a constellation sized to cover the US+Europe latitudes (roughly 25-55N) AUTOMATICALLY covers every other land mass that shares those latitudes, and, because the band is symmetric (+/-i), the mirror-image southern latitudes too:

- 25-55N also contains: the Mediterranean and North Africa, the Middle East, all of populated China/Korea/Japan, northern India, and the entire contiguous US.
- the symmetric 25-55S band contains: southern Brazil and Argentina/Chile, South Africa, and all of populated Australia and New Zealand.
- the lower-latitude interior of the band (0-25, both sides), which a ~53-deg constellation also covers (its planes pass through these latitudes on every orbit), contains: Southeast Asia, most of India, Sub-Saharan Africa, Central America, and northern South America.

**The practical statement for the model: there is no such thing as a "US + Europe only" inclined constellation. "Cover US + Europe" is geometrically identical to "cover the global ~53-deg mid-latitude band," which is ~95% of humanity (Section 1). You pay for the band once and get Sao Paulo, Johannesburg, Mumbai, Shanghai, Jakarta, Sydney, and Buenos Aires at no additional satellite cost, because they live in the same band you already bought.** This is also why the SOC counts in [`leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md`](leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md) flagged that "you pay for the empty mid-Atlantic longitudes too, because a full orbital ring spans all longitudes anyway" (COMM-215): the flip side of that cost is that all the inhabited longitudes in the band come included. The USPTO constellation-design literature makes the populated-band-targeting explicit, with a patent family on non-geostationary constellations for "continuous coverage of northern latitudes above 25 deg and its extension to global coverage tailored to the distribution of populated land masses on earth" ([USPTO 5,931,417](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5931417)). [DERIVED from Facts A+B; corroborated by the patent literature]

## 3. Inclination choice: fly ~53 deg

### 3.1 What real systems fly (multi-source)

### 3.2 Why inclined beats polar for populated latitudes (the coverage-efficiency trade)

Three sourced facts compose the trade:

- **A satellite dwells longest at its turnaround latitude (near i), so coverage piles up there.** The sub-satellite latitude traces a sinusoid in time; at the extremes (max latitude) the rate of change of latitude passes through zero, so the satellite lingers there. A patent states it plainly: the orbital track "create[s] a higher density of satellites near the northern-most and southern-most planes of latitude as compared to near the equator" ([USPTO 11,611,392](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11611392)). So a 53-deg constellation puts its RICHEST coverage in the ~45-55 deg band, exactly where the developed-world population and economy concentrate. [FACT/DERIVED]
- **A polar constellation wastes satellite-time over the empty poles and is thinnest over the populated lower-mid latitudes.** In a Walker-Star (polar) design the orbit planes converge at the poles ([deltaV Academy](https://www.deltavacademy.com/learn/walker-constellations); [eoPortal OneWeb](https://www.eoportal.org/satellite-missions/oneweb) notes coverage "becomes sparser near the equator"). The design literature confirms: "the lowest coverage appears near the equator rather than the poles with higher inclination orbits. Consequently, although a higher inclination will improve coverage of the polar regions, coverage of lower latitudes (which usually have a higher priority) will degrade" ([ScienceDirect, inclined orbits for high-latitude comms](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0094576517307166)). [FACT]
- **The core trade (DERIVED):** a polar constellation spends a large fraction of its on-orbit satellite-seconds over the poles, where there are no customers, and is stretched thinnest over the lower-mid latitudes, where most customers are. An inclined ~53-deg constellation does the opposite: it never visits the empty poles and concentrates its dwell-weighted coverage in the populated band. For a given satellite count, the inclined design delivers more usable coverage-over-people. This is why even the global players (Telesat, Starlink) bolt an inclined ~51-53 deg sub-shell onto a polar base specifically to add capacity over the mid-latitudes. [DERIVED]

### 3.3 Inclination vs coverage latitude, and the recommendation

Service reaches HIGHER than i, because a satellite at its turnaround over latitude i still has a footprint extending poleward by the footprint half-angle. From [`leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md`](leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md) (COMM-209), the 550 km footprint half-angle is ~8.5 deg at a 25-deg elevation mask and ~15 deg at a 10-deg mask, so service reach is roughly i + (a few to ~10+ deg). Applied to 53 deg: densest coverage up to ~53 deg, usable-but-thinning reach into the low-to-mid 60s with a generous footprint, falling off for the Nordics (59-71N), northern Canada, and Alaska (61-65N). Corroborated: SpaceX's 53-deg shells maximize mid-latitude coverage "but excluding higher latitudes, including Alaska, northern Canada and northern Europe," which is exactly why SpaceX ADDED 70-deg and 97.6-deg shells ([Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2023/02/02/falcon-9-starlink-5-3-coverage/); [Wikipedia Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink)); Globalstar at 52 deg "does not cover polar areas" ([Wikipedia Globalstar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalstar)); Kuiper's 33-51.9 deg shells reach ~3-4 deg past the top shell ([SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/amazons-kuiper-constellation-gets-fcc-approval/)). [FACT for the reach relationship and exclusions; DERIVED for the applied 53-deg numbers]

**Recommendation: fly the primary D2C shell at ~53 deg inclination.** It is the validated industry standard for this exact mission (Starlink D2C and AST both 53.0; Globalstar 52; Kuiper top 51.9; Telesat capacity 50.9), it places the dwell-weighted coverage peak across CONUS and populated Europe with footprint reach into the low 60s, it avoids the polar-constellation inefficiency, and it is launch-energy-reasonable from mid-latitude US sites (relevant to the Neutron-from-Virginia work in [`research/rocket_lab/neutron/`](../rocket_lab/neutron/)). **If the Nordics / high-latitude Europe / Alaska are in scope, the proven move is to ADD a second higher-inclination shell (70 deg for the Nordics and much of Alaska, or ~97.6 deg SSO for true Arctic), NOT to raise the base inclination**, which would dilute the dwell-weighted coverage out of the dense band toward emptier high latitudes (the inefficiency to avoid). Every operator that needs both serves both with two shells, not one compromise inclination. [DERIVED recommendation]

## 4. The 95% (not 100%) coverage target

### 4.1 Terrestrial mobile already stops at ~95% of population, not 100% of land

This is the realistic target shape, and it is set by economics, not by an arbitrary cutoff. The ITU reports that ~95% of the world population lives inside a mobile-broadband footprint while only ~80-87% of LAND is covered (the UK, for instance, gives 99% of premises outdoor coverage but covers only 80-87% of its landmass across operators), because **coverage is measured by population, and the remaining ~5% of people are rural, remote, off-grid, low-density, and overwhelmingly in developing regions** ("almost every person without access to a mobile broadband network lives in a rural area of a developing country"; "55% of people without access to 4G live in low-income countries") ([ITU Facts and Figures 2023](https://www.itu.int/itu-d/reports/statistics/2023/10/10/ff23-mobile-network-coverage/)). The coverage gap has barely moved (~4 points since 2018) precisely because the last few percent are the hardest and least economic to reach: power supply is the dominant cost in remote deployment, with a majority of the new sites needed to close the gap lying far from the grid ([ITU](https://www.itu.int/itu-d/reports/statistics/2022/11/24/ff22-mobile-network-coverage/); [GSMA State of Mobile Internet Connectivity](https://www.gsma.com/r/somic/)). [FACT, multi-source]

### 4.2 What 95%-of-population vs 100%-of-band does to the satellite count

The terrestrial cost curve has a direct constellation analogue: the last few percent of coverage is disproportionately expensive, and it is expensive for the SAME geometric reason in both media (the hardest edges are the sparsest, highest-latitude, lowest-density ground). The constellation-design literature confirms the shape: constellations typically deliver ~90-95% coverage with their core fleet, and pushing to full coverage means "adding more satellites at additional cost," specifically "adding a few satellites at polar inclinations" or "another plane," i.e. the 95%-to-99%+ jump is paid in extra shells and planes targeting the empty edges ([CBO, Large Constellations of Low-Altitude Satellites](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/59175); [Portillo et al., serving uncovered and underserved communities](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/sat.1464); [Nature Communications, low-cost near-continuous global coverage](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-13865-0)). [FACT/DERIVED, multi-source]

So the qualitative effect on the count:

- **A 95%-of-population D2C target is a SINGLE inclined ~53-deg band, sized to the streets-of-coverage FLOOR.** That is the few-hundred-satellite US+Europe-band number from [`leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md`](leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md) (US+Europe band ~130-450 single-coverage, COMM-215; near-global mid-latitude band ~290-960, COMM-216), because covering the 53-deg band IS covering ~95% of people (Sections 1-2). No polar shells, no high-latitude additions: one band, one inclination, the SOC count.
- **Chasing 100% of the latitude band, or the last few percent of people, is exactly the move that breaks that economy.** It adds (a) higher-inclination shells to reach the Nordics / Alaska / the sparse high-latitude edges (Section 3.3), and/or (b) the multi-fold coverage multiplier (the 2-4x on the SOC floor for handover/diversity/fault-tolerance, COMM-225 in [`leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md`](leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md)) needed to guarantee service at the weakest edges. Either way the count climbs well above the single-band floor for the last few percent of users, the same disproportionate last-percent cost the ITU and the constellation literature both describe. [DERIVED]

**The clean planning statement: target 95% of population, which is one inclined ~53-deg band at the SOC floor (a few hundred satellites for the US+Europe-equivalent band, COMM-215/216). The last few percent of people and the empty high-latitude edges of the band are what would push the count up through polar-shell additions and multi-fold multipliers, so they should be an explicit, separately-priced scope decision, not a default.** This is the coverage-geography counterpart to the COVERAGE-vs-CAPACITY finding in [`leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md`](leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md): coverage of where the people are is cheap (one band, the floor); the expensive marginal satellites go to the sparse edges (the last few percent) and to capacity over dense ground (COMM-224), not to the bulk of the population. [DERIVED]

## Open questions / uncertainties

- **The band fractions are reconstructed, not quoted.** The +/-55 / +/-45 / +/-35 cumulative population fractions are computed from multi-sourced anchors (50% in 20-40N; ~88% North; median 27N; ~2-3% north-of-50N) plus WorldPop per-degree bands, not transcribed from a published cumulative curve. The +/-55 figure (~96-97%) is high-confidence; +/-45 (~92-95%) medium; +/-35 (~78-83%) the softest because it cuts through the densest 30-40N belt. A definitive curve would require integrating the SEDAC GPWv4 raster by latitude directly, or obtaining Rankin's underlying CSV (radicalcartography.net was unreachable in this pass, SSL cert expired).
- **AST inclination detail is partly single-source.** The 53-deg primary inclination is corroborated (Gunter's flown orbit plus the FCC filing), but the exact ~98-deg SSO-shell figure and the per-shell satellite split rest on a single FCC document (DA-26-391) that did not parse cleanly; treat them as single-source pending a clean read.
- **Footprint margin into the high latitudes is altitude/elevation-dependent.** The "53 deg reaches into the low 60s" claim uses the 10-deg-mask footprint; at a quality 25-deg mask the reach is smaller (53 + ~8.5 deg). The exact poleward reach of a 53-deg band is a function of the elevation mask the service accepts, which couples to the link budget, not settled here.
- **This doc frames the count, it does not size it.** The buildable satellite counts live in [`leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md`](leo_constellation_coverage_minimums.md) (SOC floors, slightly conservative vs optimized Walker). This doc establishes WHERE the people are, WHY the band covers them, WHICH inclination, and the 95%-vs-100% target shape; it does not produce a new count.
- **Demand weighting is directional, not quantified.** Section 1.5 establishes that mobile demand is MORE mid-latitude-concentrated than raw population, but a precise subscriber-weighted latitude curve was not built (it would need ITU/GSMA per-country subscriber counts mapped to country centroids). The qualitative conclusion (covering +/-55 captures essentially all mobile demand) is robust; a quantified demand-weighted band fraction is an open item.
