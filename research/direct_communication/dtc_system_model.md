## 0. The governing rule, in one paragraph (read this first)

**First, two service tiers, defined explicitly (this model uses these labels throughout, and they are NOT the same word as the source ladder's "broadband"):**

- **Tier 1, "4G-grade DTC data": ~2 to 10 Mbps to a bare phone (text + voice + real data), the rung above texting/thin-data.** This is the Starlink V3 DTC target class and the source ladder's "~25 m^2 = voice/low-rate data" rung (COMM-298/308 call this "voice/few-Mbps," explicitly NOT broadband). It is reachable at a MODERATE aperture (~25 m^2, V3-class).
- **Tier 2, "true broadband-to-phone": ~100+ Mbps to a bare phone (AST-class).** The source ladder puts this at ~60+ m^2 (AST Block 1 ~64 m^2 demonstrated ~21 Mbps; Block 2 ~223 m^2 up to ~120 Mbps/cell), COMM-298/302. A ~25 m^2 aperture does NOT reach this rung at any buildable altitude.

When this doc earlier said "DTC broadband" it meant Tier 1 (the V3 4G-grade rung), which the source ladder does not call broadband. That relabel is now made explicit and is no longer presented as something COMM-298/308 say.

**The governing rule.** The data rate a satellite can deliver to an unmodified phone is set by four satellite-side levers, with the phone held fixed and weak: satellite antenna aperture, satellite power, path loss (which falls as you fly lower), and owned spectrum bandwidth. APERTURE and owned SPECTRUM (bandwidth) are the strong levers; ALTITUDE is a WEAK lever (dropping 550 km to 350 km buys only ~3.5 to 4 dB, far less than the ~9.5 dB aperture span from ~25 m^2 to ~223 m^2, or the ~22 dB from the ~1 m^2 texting floor to ~223 m^2); and satellite COUNT is NOT a per-phone-rate lever at all (it sets coverage continuity and total system capacity). The levers that let a moderate ~25 m^2 aperture deliver Tier-1 4G-grade DTC data are the APERTURE itself plus OWNED SPECTRUM; flying low contributes only a few dB on top, so altitude does NOT make a small antenna act like a big one. There is a hard FLOOR: below a moderate aperture (~25 m^2, V3-class) you cannot deliver even Tier-1 4G-grade DTC data to a bare phone (a ~1 m^2 aperture only texts), and no altitude reduction and no number of satellites lifts the per-phone rate above that floor. So "a smaller antenna works if you fly lower" is TRUE only in the narrow sense that a moderate ~25 m^2 aperture reaches Tier 1 (mainly on aperture + spectrum, with low altitude a minor help), and FALSE both below the ~25 m^2 floor and as a route to Tier-2 broadband (no buildable altitude turns ~25 m^2 into the ~60+ m^2 Tier-2 rung). The aperture ladder is correct but is drawn at a fixed altitude; altitude must always be stated alongside it, while remembering altitude is the small term. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-315, COMM-318, COMM-320]

## 1. The governing link budget (what sets the per-phone rate)

A DTC link closes when the signal received at the far end exceeds the decode threshold by the required margin, in BOTH directions (the phone's weak uplink to the satellite, and the satellite's downlink to the phone). The standard Friis / satcom link-budget terms ([`dtc_antenna_aperture_tradeoff.md`](dtc_antenna_aperture_tradeoff.md) Section 1, COMM-296):

```
P_rx (dBm) = EIRP_tx (dBm) - FSPL (dB) + G_rx (dBi) - losses
FSPL (dB)  = 20 log10(d) + 20 log10(f) + 92.45      (d in km, f in GHz)   <- path loss, grows with distance d
G          = 4 pi eta A / lambda^2                   (antenna gain from aperture area A, efficiency eta)
Shannon: C = B x log2(1 + SNR)                        (capacity from bandwidth B and signal-to-noise SNR)
```

[SOURCED, formulas: standard link budget; the `G = 4 pi eta A / lambda^2` form is stated in the SpaceX Gen2 DTC FCC technical narrative and the direct-to-device literature, per COMM-296.]

The four levers, and why the phone is not one of them:

| Lever | Relationship | Direction it helps | Owner of the claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Satellite antenna aperture A** | gain `G = 4 pi eta A / lambda^2`; bigger A raises BOTH downlink EIRP and uplink receive sensitivity (G/T) | larger A -> higher rate | COMM-295, COMM-296 [SOURCED] |
| **Satellite power** | raises downlink EIRP (EIRP = Tx power x gain) | more power -> higher downlink rate | COMM-295 [SOURCED] (power is the second EIRP term alongside gain) |
| **Path loss (a function of altitude)** | `FSPL = 20 log10(d) + ...`; the path eats ~150+ dB to LEO; FSPL grows with the square of slant distance d, and d falls as altitude falls | lower altitude (shorter d) -> less path loss -> higher rate | COMM-294 (the ~153 dB / 25 dB deficit), COMM-209/217 (slant range falls with altitude) [SOURCED] |
| **Owned spectrum bandwidth B** | Shannon `C = B x log2(1 + SNR)`; rate scales linearly in B and only logarithmically in SNR | more owned MHz -> proportionally higher rate | COMM-229/236 (quantity), COMM-296/Shannon [SOURCED] |

**The phone is fixed and weak, and that is the whole problem.** A standard smartphone transmits ~0.2 W (~23 dBm) into a ~0 dBi near-omnidirectional antenna (EIRP ~23 dBm), and SAR limits plus the lack of physical space for a high-gain element mean none of this can be improved (COMM-293) [SOURCED]. In the uplink at ~1900 MHz to LEO, free-space path loss is ~153 dB, so the phone's signal arrives at the satellite at ~-130 dBm against an LTE-QPSK decode floor of ~-105 dBm, a ~25 dB deficit the SATELLITE ALONE must close (COMM-294) [SOURCED, single-source on the exact dB]. Because the phone cannot change, the satellite-side levers (A, power, altitude, B) are the only ones available.

**Why path loss is a real lever, but a WEAK one.** FSPL grows with `20 log10(d)`, i.e. with the square of the slant distance. The coverage doc's slant ranges make the size of this lever concrete: at 550 km / 25 deg the slant range to the footprint edge is ~1,125 km, and at 350 km / 25 deg it is ~750 km (COMM-209) [SOURCED]. Dropping the operating range from ~1,125 km to ~750 km cuts FSPL by `20 log10(1125/750) ~ 3.5 dB` at the footprint edge, and by a bit more at higher elevation angles where the path is closer to straight up (overhead slant approaches the bare altitude, ~550 km vs ~350 km, a `20 log10(550/350) ~ 3.9 dB` reduction). A dB of path-loss reduction is the same currency as a dB of aperture gain (in the power-limited regime DTC handset links sit in, +1 dB moves the rate by a factor ~1.26 and +2 dB by ~1.585; the corpus's earlier "1 dB ~ 1.58x" was the +2 dB factor mislabeled, corrected in COMM-356), **but the two levers are not the same size: the entire 550-to-350 km altitude span buys only ~3.5 to 4 dB, whereas the aperture span from ~25 m^2 to ~223 m^2 is ~10 log10(223/25) ~ 9.5 dB, and from the ~1 m^2 texting floor to ~223 m^2 is ~22 dB (COMM-299).** So altitude is a minor contributor that can nudge a rate within a tier, not a lever that moves a satellite between aperture rungs. This magnitude gap is the load-bearing fact behind Section 3: aperture (plus owned spectrum) sets the rung, altitude only trims it. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-316]

## 2. The levers that set per-phone rate, and the one that does NOT

**This distinction is load-bearing. State it plainly: aperture, altitude, and spectrum set the per-phone RATE; satellite COUNT does not.**

### 2.1 Aperture, altitude, spectrum -> per-phone rate (link closure and per-cell capacity)

These three set how good the link to a single phone is, and how much capacity sits over a single cell:

- **Aperture** sets gain (COMM-296), which sets both link closure and (via narrower beams / higher SNR) per-cell capacity. The revealed ladder, AT FIXED ALTITUDE: ~1 m^2 = intermittent SMS, ~25 m^2 = a few Mbps/beam (Tier 1, "voice/low-rate data" in the source's words), ~60+ m^2 = true broadband-to-phone (Tier 2; AST Block 1 ~64 m^2, Block 2 ~223 m^2) (COMM-298, COMM-308) [SOURCED]. Note the source ladder places broadband at ~60+ m^2, not at 25 m^2; the ~25 m^2 rung is Tier-1 data, not broadband.
- **Altitude** sets path loss (Section 1): lower is better for the link, all else equal.
- **Spectrum** sets the Shannon bandwidth term: per-phone and per-cell rate scale linearly with owned MHz (COMM-296 Shannon; the quantity/cost of that spectrum is COMM-229/236/245) [SOURCED].

### 2.2 Satellite COUNT -> coverage (continuity) and TOTAL system capacity, NOT per-phone rate

The coverage doc settles this directly. Adding satellites operates in two regimes, neither of which is "raise the rate to one phone":

- **Below the coverage floor**, added satellites buy CONTINUITY (time-of-day and geographic coverage). A single LEO satellite is overhead only ~2 to 8 minutes per pass (COMM-211), so continuous service needs a string of satellites per plane and multiple planes; the floor is reached when every point always sees at least one satellite above the elevation mask (COMM-224) [SOURCED].
- **Above the floor**, added satellites over the same ground buy TOTAL CAPACITY (more beams, more frequency reuse, more simultaneous users), governed by Shannon x number of beams over the area (COMM-224) [SOURCED]. This raises how many phones can be served at once and the aggregate bits, NOT the ceiling rate deliverable to any one phone.

**The founder's own hypothesis, already verified in the corpus:** "the primary effect of adding satellites is COVERAGE up to a floor, then CAPACITY beyond it" is CORRECT (COMM-227) [SOURCED]. The per-phone rate ceiling is set by the per-satellite link (aperture x altitude x spectrum), and no number of satellites moves it. A constellation of a thousand ~1 m^2 texting satellites still only texts each phone; it just texts more phones, more continuously. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-317]

**The one nuance (does not change the rule):** more satellites can add MULTI-fold coverage (2+ satellites visible at once), which enables handover, diversity, and beam combining, and at the margin a few dB of array-combining gain. That is a second-order continuity/robustness benefit, not the primary lever, and it costs ~2 to 4x the single-coverage floor (COMM-225) [SOURCED]. It does not turn a texting aperture into a broadband one. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-317]

## 3. Aperture sets the tier, altitude only trims it (the explicit fix for the prior flip)

This is the section the whole document exists for. It states the single rule that resolves "a smaller antenna works if you fly lower" against "broadband-to-phone needs a giant antenna." The resolution is NOT an aperture-altitude trade (altitude is too weak for that, ~4 dB); it is that the two claims are about two different service tiers, and APERTURE plus owned SPECTRUM, not altitude, set which tier a satellite reaches.

### 3.1 The two claims that flipped, and why both readings came from the same ladder

- **First (correct) instinct:** a smaller antenna works if you fly lower, but you need many more satellites. The "smaller antenna works" half is really an APERTURE-plus-SPECTRUM point (a ~25 m^2 aperture on owned spectrum reaches Tier-1 4G-grade data); flying lower adds only a few dB on top (Section 1.2). The "many more satellites" half is the coverage-floor consequence of a smaller footprint at low altitude (COMM-217: lower altitude roughly doubles-to-triples the coverage floor) [SOURCED].
- **Second (wrong) claim:** broadband-to-phone needs a 64 to 223 m^2 antenna, full stop. For TRUE broadband (Tier 2, ~100+ Mbps) this is actually CORRECT per the source ladder (COMM-298: broadband starts ~60 m^2). The error was applying it to the V3 ~25 m^2 fleet, which targets Tier-1 4G-grade data, not Tier-2 broadband. The two claims never conflicted once the tiers are separated; they were conflated by calling the V3 Tier-1 target "broadband."

Both readings came from the same ladder because the ladder is stated WITHOUT an altitude column AND with a single "service class" column that blurs the two tiers. AST (the ~64 to 223 m^2 datapoints) is the HIGHER-altitude system, planning a ~725 to 740 km operational constellation (its BlueWalker 3 / early BlueBird demonstrations flew at the lower ~507 to 523 km, but the PLANNED shell is ~725 to 740 km, COMM-357) and reaches Tier-2 broadband; Starlink's V3 dedicated DTC fleet (the ~25 m^2 datapoint) flies very low (~326 to 335 km, COMM-277) and reaches Tier-1 4G-grade data. The ladder mixes both altitudes and both tiers without labeling them, so reasoning from memory silently assumed one altitude and one service meaning. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-318]

### 3.2 The reconciliation: aperture (plus spectrum) sets the tier; altitude only trims it

From Section 1, the link closes on `G_aperture - FSPL_altitude` (plus power and spectrum). The two systems sit at different APERTURE rungs serving different TIERS, and the altitude difference between them is a minor term, not the thing doing the work:

- **AST, big aperture, HIGHER altitude, Tier-2 broadband:** ~223 m^2 (Block 2) at a PLANNED ~725 to 740 km operational shell (the BW3 / early demos flew at ~507 to 523 km, but the planned constellation is ~725 to 740 km, COMM-357), delivering true broadband-to-phone (up to ~120 Mbps/cell) (COMM-300, COMM-301) [SOURCED]. AST reaches the higher tier at the LARGER aperture and the HIGHER altitude, which is the opposite of an "altitude rescues a small antenna" story.
- **Starlink V3 DTC, moderate aperture, LOWER altitude, Tier-1 data:** a ~25 m^2-class aperture at ~326 to 335 km delivering the 4G-LTE-equivalent target (text + voice + data, ~100 Mbps peak / ~2 to 10 Mbps sustained per the reporting) (COMM-277, COMM-278, COMM-303) [SOURCED; the Mbps figures are reported projections, flagged in COMM-278].

The altitude delta between the two PLANNED architectures is ~400 km (AST ~725 to 740 km vs Starlink ~326 to 335 km), which on the path-loss curve is ~6.7 to 7.1 dB overhead (still smaller than the aperture delta), while the aperture delta (~25 m^2 to ~223 m^2) is ~9.5 dB and the tier gap (Tier 1 to Tier 2) is larger still. (At the BW3 demo altitude of ~507 to 523 km the delta was only ~180 km / under ~4 dB, but AST's planned operational shell is higher, COMM-357.) So altitude is NOT what separates the two systems; the APERTURE rung is, and AST actually sits at the harder corner (bigger antenna AND higher planned altitude) because Tier-2 broadband demands it. The corrected, larger altitude gap STRENGTHENS this: AST does not fly low, so the low-orbit advantages (the path-loss trim and, decisively, the latency edge of Section 3.6 below) belong to the LOW-flying entrant, not to AST.

**They deliver DIFFERENT tiers, and they sit at different APERTURE rungs, not merely different altitudes.** The existence proof that a MODERATE (~25 m^2) aperture reaches Tier-1 4G-grade DTC data is Starlink's own dedicated fleet: SpaceX did not put a 223 m^2 antenna on those satellites, but the lever that gets a ~25 m^2 array to Tier 1 is the aperture plus OWNED SPECTRUM (~65 MHz from EchoStar) and constellation scale, with low altitude a minor (~4 dB) help on top (COMM-278, COMM-280) [SOURCED]. It does not reach Tier-2 broadband, and no lower altitude would get it there. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-319]

### 3.3 The floor, and why altitude is the wrong lever to reach for

Altitude is the weak term. Lowering it buys back only ~3.5 to 4 dB across the whole 550-to-350 km span (Section 1.2), so it cannot move a satellite between aperture rungs or between tiers; it can only nudge a rate within a tier. What actually gets a ~25 m^2 aperture to Tier-1 4G-grade data is the APERTURE itself plus OWNED SPECTRUM (more MHz, Shannon-linear), with the few dB of low altitude a minor help. And no lever rescues a sub-floor aperture: a ~1 m^2 aperture is ~22 dB short of AST-class (COMM-299), and no buildable LEO altitude reduction delivers anything close to that (~4 dB total). So:

- **At and above the moderate floor (~25 m^2, V3-class):** a moderate aperture reaches Tier-1 4G-grade DTC data, mainly on aperture + owned spectrum (low altitude trims a few dB). "A smaller antenna works" is TRUE here for TIER 1, and it is the aperture and spectrum, not the altitude, doing the work.
- **Below the moderate floor (toward ~1 m^2):** nothing works. A ~1 m^2 aperture only texts, even from low orbit, because the aperture deficit is tens of dB and altitude buys back only a few (COMM-308: a satellite antenna materially below ~1 m^2 cannot close the bare-handset link at a useful service level; ~25 m^2 is the voice/few-Mbps rung) [SOURCED]. More satellites do not raise the per-phone rate (Section 2).
- **Above the moderate floor, toward Tier-2 broadband (~60+ m^2):** altitude still does not substitute for aperture. Reaching true broadband (~100+ Mbps) requires the ~60 to 223 m^2 aperture (COMM-298); a ~25 m^2 array does not get there from any buildable altitude, because the gap is many dB and altitude offers only ~4 dB.

### 3.4 The single reconciled rule (the governing statement)

> **APERTURE plus OWNED SPECTRUM set which service tier a DTC satellite reaches; ALTITUDE is a weak (~3.5 to 4 dB across 550 to 350 km) trim, and SATELLITE COUNT sets coverage and total capacity, never per-phone rate. A moderate ~25 m^2 (V3-class) aperture reaches Tier-1 4G-grade DTC data (~2 to 10 Mbps) on aperture + owned spectrum, with low altitude a minor help; true Tier-2 broadband-to-phone (~100+ Mbps) requires the ~60 to 223 m^2 aperture (AST-class) and no buildable altitude substitutes for it. Below the ~25 m^2 floor (toward ~1 m^2) you only text no matter how low you fly. The aperture ladder is correct but is drawn at a fixed altitude, so altitude must be stated alongside it, while remembering altitude is the small term.** [SYNTHESIS; COMM-320, the governing rule]

A compact way to hold it: **aperture (plus owned spectrum) picks the tier; altitude trims a few dB within a tier and never moves you between aperture rungs; satellite count is never a per-phone-rate lever at all.**

### 3.5 The grounded Tier-1 operating point (the single-phone rate the product is built around)

Sections 0 through 3.4 fix the relationships qualitatively. This subsection pins the ONE point inside Tier 1 the product is designed around: a SINGLE-PHONE downlink of ~25 to 50 Mbps, delivered by a MIDDLE aperture (~50 m^2, between Starlink's ~25 m^2 and AST's ~223 m^2) plus LOW orbit (~350 to 550 km) plus realistic OWNED spectrum. It grounds in [`dtc_per_phone_rate_and_latency.md`](dtc_per_phone_rate_and_latency.md) (COMM-336..355).

**First, resolve per-cell vs per-phone (the distinction the operating point turns on).** The published DTC throughput headlines are PER-CELL / PER-BEAM (a shared pool), NOT per-single-phone:

- AST's "up to 120 Mbps" is explicitly "per coverage cell across more than 2,000 cells" (AST's own words), shared across the hundreds-to-thousands of phones in a ~30 to 48 km beam (COMM-336) [SOURCED].
- Starlink's measured ~3.1 Mbps is PER BEAM, which the source paper states verbatim is "an upper-bound estimate of the throughput per connection... a single user occupying the full bandwidth of the beam" (COMM-337) [SOURCED].

A satellite beam serves users by a time-division scheduler, one phone per resource block at an instant, exactly like a terrestrial cell. So the per-cell figure and the best-case single-phone figure CONVERGE when one phone is alone in the cell (it is scheduled across the whole pool, so per-cell-when-alone equals the single-phone peak), and DIVERGE under load (the pool time-shares across all active phones, and the analyst figure for the per-user rate in a busy cell is ~1 Mbps or less) (COMM-338, COMM-339, COMM-340) [SOURCED/analyst]. **The single-phone PEAK is the per-cell capacity; the single-phone AVERAGE falls with concurrent users.** The product target (~25 to 50 Mbps to one lightly-loaded phone) is the PEAK / sole-occupant quantity, which is the correct thing to size aperture and bandwidth against, and it is distinct from both the per-cell aggregate (AST 120 Mbps/cell) and the busy-cell per-user average (~1 Mbps). [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-358, COMM-359]

**The aperture that delivers the single-phone rate: ~50 m^2 is enough, and ~64 to 100 m^2 is NOT needed for the RATE.** The empirical anchor is almost exactly this case: AST BlueWalker 3, a ~64 m^2 array, demonstrated up to ~21 Mbps to a single phone (with two undisclosed quantities, the simultaneous device count and the test channel bandwidth, both UNKNOWN) (COMM-338) [SOURCED, single-device read]. A ~50 m^2 array is only +1.07 dB smaller in gain (10 log10(64/50)), so in aperture terms the middle array is essentially the BW3 array (COMM-360) [DERIVED]. Dropping to low orbit (~350 to 400 km) buys back ~3.5 to 3.9 dB of path loss versus AST's demo altitude, MORE than offsetting the shrink (a net ~+2.4 to +2.8 dB versus BW3 even after the array is made smaller). Rate then scales linearly with owned bandwidth (Shannon B term): ~17 MHz at 3 bps/Hz, ~20 MHz at 2.5, or ~25 MHz at 2 bps/Hz reaches 50 Mbps to one phone, well inside AST's own 40 MHz / 120 Mbps-per-cell envelope on a comparable aperture (COMM-344) [DERIVED].

> **THE TIER-1 OPERATING POINT.** A ~50 m^2 aperture at low orbit (~350 to 550 km) on ~20 to 40 MHz of OWNED spectrum delivers ~25 to 50 Mbps to a SINGLE phone (the peak / lightly-loaded rate). **You do NOT need ~64 to 100 m^2 for the single-phone RATE; the extra aperture above ~50 to 64 m^2 buys per-cell CAPACITY (more concurrent users, smaller cells, more beams), not a higher peak to one handset.** This sits at the Tier-1 rung of the governing rule (Section 3.4, COMM-320): aperture plus owned spectrum set the rate, altitude trims a few dB, and ~50 m^2 sits above the ~25 m^2 Tier-1 floor and below the ~60+ m^2 Tier-2 broadband rung. [SYNTHESIS; COMM-361, the operating point]

Two honest soft spots carry from the source (Section 2.4 there): the BW3 anchor's two undisclosed quantities (if its ~21 Mbps was already a multi-device aggregate, the single-phone start point is lower and the chain needs more owned bandwidth), and the linear-bandwidth assumption (spreading fixed power over more bandwidth lowers SNR-per-Hz; the ~+2.4 to +2.8 dB net from low orbit is the margin that makes a wider channel plausible, but the test-bandwidth number is not pinned). These are flagged, not sealed (COMM-348) [UNKNOWN / ESTIMATE].

### 3.6 Latency: a structural Tier-1 advantage that belongs to the low-flying entrant

Low orbit is not only the path-loss trim of Section 1.2; it is a qualitatively decisive advantage for a PHONE service because of latency, and (given the corrected AST altitude, Section 3.2) it is an advantage of the LOW-flying entrant, not of AST:

- **Propagation.** A low-LEO DTC link (~350 to 550 km) carries ~5 to 10 ms of round-trip propagation (phone-sat-gateway-sat-phone) and ~20 to 50 ms typical end-to-end; GEO carries ~240 to 280 ms of propagation (a single GEO uplink leg alone is ~120 ms) and ~600 to 700 ms measured, a ~30 to 50x physics gap that no better silicon closes (COMM-350, COMM-351) [DERIVED floors / FACT measured].
- **The product threshold.** ITU-T G.114 puts interactive voice "preferred" up to 150 ms one-way and "unacceptable" above 400 ms. A low-LEO link spends only ~5 to 10 ms on the satellite hop, leaving essentially the entire "good" band free for codec, jitter buffer, and backhaul (vendors quote 20 to 50 ms total), so a satellite call feels like a normal mobile call; a GEO two-hop voice path reaches ~250 to 300 ms+ one-way, into the degraded-to-unacceptable band. The field proof is the decades-old Iridium (LEO, natural-feeling calls) versus Inmarsat/Thuraya (GEO, perceptible delay) contrast (COMM-352) [SOURCED thresholds / DERIVED budget].
- **Whose edge it is.** AST's PLANNED ~725 to 740 km shell (Section 3.2, COMM-357) is NOT low orbit, so this latency edge is the LOW-flying entrant's, not AST's. It is a structural reason the Tier-1 product flies low BEYOND the ~3.5 to 4 dB of path loss low orbit buys. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-363]

(No MEASURED end-to-end DTC latency, for Starlink DTC or AST, is published; the ~20 to 50 ms is the broadband-LEO measured figure plus the propagation floor, not a measured DTC number. The ~5 to 10 ms propagation floor is solid physics; the end-to-end DTC latency is a named evidence gap, COMM-353.) [UNKNOWN]

## 4. The two architectures (different tiers, opposite ends of the aperture-vs-count trade)

There are two proven architectures for putting service on a bare phone, and they reach DIFFERENT tiers. AST (Architecture (a)) reaches Tier-2 broadband (~100+ Mbps) with a giant aperture; Starlink/Flatellite (Architecture (b)) reaches Tier-1 4G-grade data (~2 to 10 Mbps) with a moderate aperture and many satellites. They are the two ends of the aperture-vs-count tradeoff (COMM-306, COMM-307), the same link-budget physics worked at different APERTURE rungs (Section 3), serving different service tiers.

### 4.3 Why both land at comparable SYSTEM capacity from opposite ends

AST concentrates capacity into few high-aperture nodes; Starlink/Flatellite spreads it across many low-aperture nodes. Per the independent ~100x-per-satellite (AST) and the ~100 to 1,000x-more-satellites (Starlink) figures, both can reach comparable AGGREGATE DTC capacity by opposite routes (COMM-306) [SOURCED/ESTIMATE; the netting is reasoned, not directly measured]. This is comparable TOTAL bits across the system, NOT comparable per-phone tier: AST delivers Tier-2 broadband to each served phone, Starlink/Flatellite delivers Tier-1 4G-grade data, and aggregate-capacity parity does not change that per-phone difference (Section 2's rate-vs-capacity split). The difference that matters for Neutron is launch economics: for a given launcher, many-small has far lower launch cost per satellite (COMM-307) [SOURCED].

## 5. Decision-relevant numbers for a Neutron DTC business

The product, the satellites-per-launch, the constellation size, and the per-satellite capacity, each tagged. **This business is Architecture (b): moderate-aperture, low-orbit, many-satellite, Tier-1 4G-grade DTC data (not Tier-2 AST-class broadband).**

### 5.1 Product

- **Tier-1 DTC data to an unmodified phone**, at the now-grounded SINGLE-PHONE operating point: **~25 to 50 Mbps to one lightly-loaded phone** from a ~50 m^2 aperture at low orbit (~350 to 550 km) on ~20 to 40 MHz of owned spectrum (Section 3.5, COMM-361). This is a step ABOVE the ~25 m^2 V3 rung's ~2 to 10 Mbps sustained target (COMM-278) and is reached at the ~50 m^2 middle aperture, NOT the ~25 m^2 floor. It remains a Tier-1 product (the rung above texting/thin-data, below the ~60+ m^2 Tier-2 broadband rung). The ~25 to 50 Mbps is the single-phone PEAK / sole-occupant rate; the per-user average under load is lower (~1 Mbps in a busy cell, the per-cell pool shared, Section 3.5) [SYNTHESIS, COMM-359/361].
- **NOT texting** (that is the ~1 m^2 Lynk floor, COMM-298), **NOT Tier-2 broadband-to-phone** (the AST-class ~100+ Mbps PER-CELL rung needs the ~60 to 223 m^2 aperture and buys per-cell CAPACITY, a different architecture, COMM-298/300), and **NOT broadband-to-home** (the dish-served case where the customer's dish supplies the gain and the satellite antenna is small, COMM-311) [SOURCED]. The product also carries a structural LATENCY advantage (low-orbit ~5 to 10 ms propagation, inside ITU-T G.114's good band, which AST's planned ~725 to 740 km shell does not have, Section 3.6).

### 5.2 Satellites per Neutron

| Case | Satellites/Neutron | Basis | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Default (V3-class)** | **~6 to 8** | A V3-class DTC slab is mass-bound on Neutron; the fit corpus puts a ~1,900 kg V3-class broadband slab at ~5/Neutron (COMM-286), and a DTC variant in the same mass class lands ~6 to 8 as a working default | [SOURCED basis COMM-286; ESTIMATE for the 6 to 8 DTC band] |
| **Optimistic ceiling** | **16** | Flatellite's render-read ~16/Neutron at an UNVERIFIED ~800 kg (COMM-258, COMM-260) | [ESTIMATE, single-source render-read; mass UNKNOWN, COMM-253] |

**The 16 figure is UNVERIFIED, not implausible.** The reason to distrust it is its provenance: ~16/Neutron rides entirely on the ~800 kg Flatellite mass, which is a single third-party analyst's render-read that the author himself hedges ("could be ~200 kg less," "may be more fiction than fact"), COMM-253/256 [ESTIMATE, single-source]. Rocket Lab has published no Flatellite mass, power, or dimensions; the apparent multi-outlet spread all traces to that one render-read (COMM-256). So 16 is soft because the input is unverified single-source, full stop. (This is NOT a "would out-design SpaceX" argument: the ~1,900 kg V3 is a full broadband bus carrying a ~1 Tbps Ku/Ka broadband payload PLUS the ~25 m^2 D2C array PLUS ~60 m of solar (COMM-274), while a Flatellite pitched purely at D2C/5G-NTN (COMM-262) carries no broadband payload and could legitimately be much lighter without out-designing anything, because it is doing less. A D2C-only flat-sat mass is genuinely unknown, so the V3-mass comparison is apples-to-oranges and is not the basis for the skepticism.) The honest reading: 16 is achievable only at the unverified light mass; until Rocket Lab publishes a Flatellite mass, it cannot be a planning number. **Carry ~6 to 8 as the default and 16 as an optimistic, UNVERIFIED ceiling.** [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-321, COMM-322]

**The aperture cliff (decisive once the operating point is ~50 m^2, not ~25 m^2).** The counts above are for a ~25 m^2 V3-class slab that fits the fairing FLAT and is mass/volume-bound. The Tier-1 operating point (Section 3.5) chose a ~50 m^2 aperture, and that aperture EXCEEDS Neutron's ~5.5 m fairing as a flat panel (a 50 m^2 square is ~7.07 m on a side), so it must FOLD, and VOLUME (the folded stowed bulk) binds. An independent flat-or-fold calc (validated on the known Falcon 9 / Starlink counts: 58 vs 50-60, 21 vs 21-24, both mass-bound) sweeps the aperture and finds VOLUME binds at every DTC aperture size, with a CLIFF at the flat-to-fold transition:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ~25 m^2 (Starlink-class) | FLAT (~5.0 m side, fits ~5.5 m fairing) | ~6 | (baseline) | volume, flat-stack | [calc, this sweep] |
| **~50 m^2 (Tier-1 operating point)** | **FOLD (~7.07 m side, exceeds fairing)** | **~3** | **halved (0.50x)** | **volume, fold** | **[calc, this sweep]** |

The Block upgrade (more mass, same fairing) barely moves these, because volume binds. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-362]

### 5.3 Constellation size

- **THOUSANDS of satellites**, because the Tier-1 product flies at a LOW DTC altitude serving dense demand. The low altitude (chosen for the few dB it adds and for capacity density, not because it is the rate lever) RAISES the coverage floor: VLEO (~330 km) shrinks each footprint, so more satellites are needed just for continuity (COMM-217, COMM-226) [SOURCED], and DTC DEMAND DENSITY needs many satellites' worth of beams over busy ground (capacity regime, COMM-224) [SOURCED]. Starlink's own answer is a dedicated up-to-15,000-satellite DTC fleet (COMM-277) [SOURCED].
- **This is NOT the ~130 to 450 broadband-altitude coverage floor.** That floor (COMM-215) is for continuity at the HIGHER broadband shell (~550 km) over US+Europe; the DTC product flies LOW, where the floor is multiples higher AND capacity over dense ground pushes the count to thousands. The few-hundred number is the continuity floor for a thin pipe at broadband altitude, not the count for a low-altitude many-satellite DTC business. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-323]

### 5.4 Capacity per satellite

- **The single-phone RATE is now resolved; per-satellite TOTAL capacity is the remaining OPEN number.** Section 3.5 pins the single-phone peak (~25 to 50 Mbps from ~50 m^2 + low orbit + ~20 to 40 MHz). What is NOT pinned is per-satellite TOTAL capacity, i.e. how many concurrent users one satellite can serve at once (the per-cell pool times the number of beams), which is a different quantity from the single-phone rate (the rate-vs-capacity split of Section 2, and the per-cell-vs-per-phone split of Section 3.5). The extra aperture above ~50 to 64 m^2 buys exactly this concurrent-user CAPACITY, not single-phone peak (COMM-361). [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-364]
- **The anchors for total capacity.** The V3 anchor is ~100 Mbps peak / ~2 to 10 Mbps sustained per phone (COMM-278, reported projection) [SOURCED, flagged], ~3.1 Mbps/beam was MEASURED in the SMS-only phase (COMM-304) [SOURCED], and the dedicated V3 DTC build is PROJECTED at ~700 Gbps/sat aggregate (COMM-280) [ESTIMATE/projection, flagged]. AST's comparison point is ~120 Mbps PER CELL across 2,000+ cells, which is per-cell concurrent-user capacity, not a single-phone rate (COMM-336/358).
- **For a Neutron/Flatellite satellite specifically, per-satellite TOTAL DTC capacity is UNKNOWN**, because Flatellite's aperture area, power, and throughput are all unpublished (COMM-270, COMM-252) [UNKNOWN]. A flat, non-deployed aperture is footprint-capped (smaller than AST's 223 m^2 unfurled array), so "more satellites per launch" does not equal "more capacity per launch" until the per-satellite aperture/power is pinned (COMM-263, COMM-270) [SOURCED on the caveat; the number itself UNKNOWN]. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-324, COMM-364]

### 5.5 The spectrum gate (decision-relevant, often forgotten)

The per-phone rate scales linearly with owned bandwidth (Section 1), so the spectrum position is a first-order lever, not a footnote. A competitive position is ~100 MHz (floor) to ~200 MHz (incumbent-matching), costing ~$32B to ~$46B (100 MHz) or ~$65B to ~$90B (200 MHz) spectrum-only across US+Europe (COMM-236, COMM-245, COMM-246) [SOURCED/DERIVED]. The realistic entrant doors are SCS lease of a carrier's existing sub-7-GHz band, or buying distressed MSS spectrum (EchoStar/Ligado), NOT FR3/6G (which is terrestrial-led and physics-hostile to a phone-to-LEO link, COMM-248) [SOURCED]. Starlink's V3 DTC capacity step is bought partly with ~65 MHz of owned EchoStar spectrum (COMM-280) [SOURCED]. A Neutron DTC business inherits this same spectrum constraint regardless of how good its satellites are. [SYNTHESIS; see COMM-325]
